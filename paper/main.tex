\begin{abstract}
LLM-enabled cyber attacks pose a realistic threat in cyberspace, and the autonomous cyber defense (ACD) is a promising technology for confronting AI-enabled attacks.
However, deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based decision-making for ACD in preventing lateral-movement and responding ransomware in IT\&OT scenarios is a time-sensitive sequential decision problem.
Existing methods typically collapse the problem into a scalarized reward or a single operating point, which ignored the disruptions of critical services, exhaustion of defending resource, and rising critical risks in hindering sophisticated attackers.

This paper presents a constrained multi-objective DRL method for ACD agents by formulating the defender with four deployable objectives: \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}.
Instead of maximizing a single reward policy, our method constructs a deployable policy set through cyber-semantic objectives and constraint-aware Pareto expansion, which different operational preferences can be realized without re-training.
We propose a two-stage Pareto-oriented framework that initializes preference-diverse policies and expands them via constraint-aware Pareto optimization to yield a deployable policy set for ACD.
By implemented in the CybORG++ simulators, our method achieves \textbf{$3.9\times$} higher hypervolume than weighted-sum scalarization, raises expected utility from $-161.57$ to $-41.92$, increases feasible deployment rate to $0.64$, and reduces mean constraint violation from $93.54$ to $0.09$, without critical compromised hosts and critical-impact events.
These experiments show that a constrained trade-off management over multi-objective policy sets can enable the ACD agent with more deployable guarantees than straightforward single-policy reward maximization.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Autonomous cyber defense is increasingly studied as a way to shorten
defender response loops under fast attacker progression. Modern
enterprise attacks often unfold after an initial foothold: the attacker
performs discovery, expands compromise, moves laterally, and may
eventually disrupt business or operational processes. Prior work on
automated and autonomous cyber defense has emphasized both the promise
of reinforcement learning and the difficulty of evaluating learned
defenders in realistic, partially observable, adversarial network
settings~\cite{vyas2023automated,palmer2023drlsurvey,standen2021cyborg}.
For critical-service enterprise networks, this difficulty is not only
algorithmic. Defensive actions are operational interventions: they may
contain attacker progress, but they may also disrupt services, consume
response resources, or create unacceptable exposure around critical
assets. In IT/OT-adjacent environments, this distinction is especially
important because operational technology security must account for
performance, reliability, and safety requirements in addition to
ordinary cybersecurity objectives~\cite{nist_sp80082r3}.

This paper argues that autonomous defense should not be treated as a
single scalar-reward maximization problem. First, real response actions
induce competing objectives: containment, business continuity, response
burden, and critical-asset protection cannot always be improved
simultaneously. Second, operational preferences are not fixed. A defender
may need a continuity-preserving policy in one incident phase and a more
aggressive containment policy in another. Third, hard operational limits
cannot be reduced to preference weights: a policy that has high average
utility may still be unacceptable if it causes excessive disruption or
allows critical-asset compromise. Existing reinforcement-learning-based
autonomous defense studies have demonstrated useful progress in cyber
ranges and reward shaping~\cite{applebaum2022bridging,foley2022autonomous,bates2023reward},
but they commonly focus on learning a single operating point or
improving average return, leaving less support for policy-set selection
under explicit operational limits.

We study autonomous defense as a constrained multi-objective
policy-archive problem. The defender operates under partial observability
and selects host-level response actions from defender-visible telemetry.
The attacker is modeled at the post-compromise stage, where the relevant
semantic progression is from enterprise foothold, to pre-critical
exposure, to realized critical breach. We optimize four operational
objectives: \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity},
\emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. The fourth
objective captures tail-risk behavior around critical assets, including
first critical hits, persistent compromise, and critical dwell, and is
kept distinct from general containment. The resulting decision object is
not a universally optimal defense policy; it is an archive of candidate
policies together with an assignment rule that selects only among
policies satisfying hard operational limits.

We propose \emph{Defense-Aware Constrained Policy-Set Learning}
(DA-CPSL). DA-CPSL learns an evaluated archive of defense policies in two
stages. Stage~1 trains preference-diverse initial policies over the four
objectives. Stage~2 extends selected archive policies toward individual
objective directions while preserving other objectives and enforcing
operational acceptance constraints. Each candidate is evaluated not only
by objective returns, but also by operational violation summaries and
replay-level semantic audit variables. At assignment time, DA-CPSL
selects a policy from the acceptable Pareto set under an operator
preference. If no candidate satisfies the specified limits, the
assignment is marked infeasible rather than forcing a high-utility but
unacceptable policy. The archive-construction structure is inspired by
policy-set multi-objective reinforcement learning, including recent
two-stage Pareto-front discovery methods~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022practical,liu2025cmorl},
but DA-CPSL changes the candidate representation, acceptance layer, and
assignment rule for autonomous defense.

We evaluate DA-CPSL in a controlled enterprise cyber-range
instantiation. The evaluation uses CybORG++ Scenario2, which builds on
the CybORG line of environments for autonomous cyber agents and provides
partial observations, post-compromise attacker progression, host-level
defensive actions, and critical-asset compromise semantics
~\cite{standen2021cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}. We use the cyber range as
an experimental realization of the threat model, not as the source of
the problem definition. Our evaluation separates three forms of evidence:
objective-space archive quality, operational assignment under shared
hard limits, and replay-level critical-risk semantics.

Across three seeds, DA-CPSL produces the strongest four-objective
policy archive among the evaluated methods. Compared with weighted-sum
scalarization, it achieves \(3.9\times\) higher hypervolume, improves
expected utility from \(-161.57\) to \(-41.92\), increases the
feasible-assignment rate to \(0.64\), and reduces mean constraint
violation from \(93.54\) to \(0.09\). Replay-level semantic audit shows
no observed critical breach or persistent critical breach for the
selected DA-CPSL policy under the fixed replay protocol, while
near-miss trajectories remain visible. These results support a bounded
claim: DA-CPSL improves pre-critical containment under the evaluated
threat model and cyber-range protocol, but it does not provide formal
safety guarantees, production-readiness guarantees, or elimination of
all cyber risk.

In summary, this paper makes four contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate autonomous defense under operational constraints
    as a constrained four-objective policy-archive problem, separating
    objective trade-offs, hard acceptance constraints, and
    preference-conditioned assignment.

    \item We introduce Critical-Asset Safety as a first-class objective
    for autonomous defense and connect it to replay-level semantic audit
    variables such as critical breach, persistent breach, first critical
    hit, and critical dwell.

    \item We propose DA-CPSL, a defense-aware constrained policy-set
    learning method that constructs an evaluated archive, extends
    policies through acceptance-aware Pareto extension, and assigns
    policies only from the acceptable Pareto set.

    \item We evaluate DA-CPSL in a controlled cyber range and show that
    it improves archive quality, operational assignment acceptability,
    and replay-level post-foothold response semantics under the stated
    threat model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Setting and Threat Model}
\label{sec:problem-threat}

\subsection{Operational Setting}
\label{subsec:operational-setting}

We consider autonomous defense for a critical-service enterprise network
under ongoing attacker progression. The defended environment contains
ordinary enterprise hosts, business-relevant services, and high-value
assets whose compromise may create unacceptable operational impact. In
IT/OT-converged or OT-adjacent organizations, some high-value assets may
support operational access or mediate access to OT-facing functions;
however, our focus is cyber-level defensive decision-making rather than
physical-process control.

This setting is inherently multi-objective. A defensive response that
improves containment may interrupt a business service, consume scarce
response capacity, or introduce operational side effects if applied too
aggressively. Conversely, delaying intervention may preserve short-term
continuity and reduce immediate response burden, but it may allow an
attacker foothold to deepen toward critical assets. The defender
therefore cannot be characterized only by average security reward; it
must manage competing objectives under hard operational acceptance
constraints.

The system boundary is the decision layer of autonomous defense: the
defender selects response actions from defender-visible observations,
and the learned archive supports policy selection under operational
preferences and hard limits. We do not design enterprise monitoring or
security-orchestration infrastructure, nor do we model physical-process
control.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{subsec:threat-model}

\paragraph{Adversary.}
The adversary has obtained an initial foothold in the enterprise
environment and attempts to progress toward high-value or critical
assets. This progression may include discovery, compromise expansion,
persistence, and lateral movement across hosts or accounts. The attacker
may eventually create impact by disrupting business or operational
processes.

We describe the relevant attacker progression through three semantic
stages. In the \emph{enterprise-foothold} stage, the attacker has
compromised non-critical enterprise assets that can support further
movement. In the \emph{pre-critical} stage, no critical asset has yet
been compromised, but the current compromise pattern creates elevated
critical-asset exposure. In the \emph{critical-breach} stage, at least
one critical asset is compromised. The defender's central task is to
contain attacker progression before the transition from pre-critical
exposure to realized critical breach.

We focus on post-compromise progression rather than the full intrusion
lifecycle. We do not model initial access, phishing, exploit generation,
supply-chain compromise, physical sabotage, or an online adaptive
attacker that observes and co-learns against the current defense policy.
These exclusions keep the threat model centered on operational response
to attacker progression.

\paragraph{Defender.}
The defender operates under partial observability. At time \(t\), the
defender receives an observation \(o_t\) derived from defender-visible
telemetry, alerts, and host-level status. The defender does not observe
the full latent cyber state, the attacker's internal plan, or future
attacker actions.

The defender selects host-level response actions such as investigation,
containment, removal, restoration, deception, or other defensive
interventions. These actions differ in both security effect and
operational side effect. For example, restoration may remove attacker
presence but disrupt a business service; deception may improve
containment but consume response resources; conservative response may
preserve short-term continuity but allow an attacker foothold to deepen.
We assume that the defender's sensing and actuation interfaces are
available within the modeled decision process; attacks that compromise
these interfaces are outside this threat model.

\paragraph{Critical assets and failure semantics.}
Critical assets are hosts, services, or access-enabling systems whose
compromise would create unacceptable operational consequences. In an
IT/OT-converged operator, such assets may include systems supporting
operational access, engineering workflows, domain services, data
historians, jump hosts, or other high-value services. We do not assume
that the autonomous defender directly controls physical processes.

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) denote the set of cyber entities,
\(\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) denote the critical-asset set, and
\(C_t\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) denote the set of compromised entities at
time \(t\). We define the critical-breach indicator as:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbb{1}\{C_t\cap\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset\}.
\label{eq:threat-critical-breach}
\end{equation}
We define the pre-critical exposure indicator as:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
C_t\cap\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
\;\wedge\;
\rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})>0
\right\},
\label{eq:threat-precritical}
\end{equation}
where \(\rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})\) is a policy-independent audit function
that measures elevated exposure around critical assets. Thus,
\(b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}=1\) denotes a pre-critical state, and
\(b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1\) denotes realized critical breach.

These semantic variables are not privileged observations given to the
selected policy. They are used to define critical-risk semantics, shape
Critical-Asset Safety, and audit whether a policy that appears strong in
average return nevertheless exhibits unacceptable tail-risk behavior
around critical assets. Persistent critical breach, first critical-hit
time, and critical dwell are formalized in
Section~\ref{sec:formulation} and Appendix~\ref{app:critical-audit}.

\subsection{Operational Preferences and Scope}
\label{subsec:operational-preferences-scope}

Organizations differ in how they trade off containment, service
continuity, response burden, and critical-asset protection. We represent
these differences through operational preferences over four objectives:
\emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity},
\emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. Preferences,
however, are not hard limits. A preference specifies how an organization
ranks acceptable trade-offs, whereas a hard operational limit specifies
behavior that must be rejected regardless of scalar utility.

Under this threat model, the research object is not a universally
optimal autonomous defender. It is a finite archive of candidate defense
policies whose members expose different trade-offs among containment,
continuity, response cost, and Critical-Asset Safety, together with a
constrained selection rule that chooses an acceptable policy under
operator-specified preferences and hard operational limits. If no
candidate satisfies the specified limits, the selection should be marked
infeasible rather than forcing an unacceptable policy choice.

Our claims are limited to constrained policy-set learning and policy
selection under the stated threat model. The operational limits abstract
business-disruption tolerance, response-resource tolerance, and
critical-asset acceptance criteria; they do not replace
organization-specific approval, escalation, legal, or OT
safety-engineering processes. We do not claim formal safety guarantees,
readiness for unsupervised production operation, or elimination of all
cyber risk.


\section{Constrained Multi-Objective Defense Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

We formalize the problem setting in
Section~\ref{sec:problem-threat} as a finite-horizon, partially
observable, constrained multi-objective defense decision problem. The
formulation separates three elements that are often collapsed into a
single scalar reward in reinforcement-learning-based defense: objective
trade-offs, hard operational acceptance constraints, and
preference-conditioned policy selection.

\subsection{Partially Observable Defense Process}
\label{subsec:defense-process}

We model autonomous defense as a finite-horizon partially observable
multi-objective decision process:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}
=
\langle
\mathcal{S},
\mathcal{O},
\mathcal{A},
P,
Z,
\mathbf{r},
\gamma,
T
\rangle .
\label{eq:defense-process}
\end{equation}
Here, \(s_t\in\mathcal{S}\) is the latent cyber state,
\(o_t\in\mathcal{O}\) is the defender-visible observation, and
\(a_t\in\mathcal{A}\) is a defensive action. The observation model is
\(Z(o_t\mid s_t)\), and the transition kernel
\(P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)\) captures attacker progression, defensive
action effects, and service-state evolution.

The defense policy acts on observation histories rather than on the
latent cyber state. We write the policy as:
\begin{equation}
a_t \sim \pi(\cdot\mid h_t),
\qquad
h_t=(o_0,a_0,o_1,a_1,\ldots,o_t),
\label{eq:history-policy}
\end{equation}
where \(h_t\) may be represented explicitly or by a recurrent policy
state. The policy is not given privileged access to the full latent
state, the attacker's internal plan, future attacker actions, or
trajectory-level audit variables.

For a policy \(\pi\), we denote the induced trajectory distribution as:
\begin{equation}
\tau \sim \mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi .
\label{eq:trajectory-distribution}
\end{equation}
Audit variables derived from latent compromise state are used for reward
construction, diagnostics, and evaluation, but they are not exposed as
policy inputs.

\subsection{Operational Objectives and Critical-Asset Safety}
\label{subsec:objectives}

Each transition produces a four-dimensional reward vector:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_t
=
\left[
r_t^{\mathrm{sec}},
r_t^{\mathrm{bus}},
r_t^{\mathrm{cost}},
r_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
\right]^\top ,
\label{eq:reward-vector}
\end{equation}
corresponding to \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business
Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. For
a policy \(\pi\), we define the expected return vector as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\gamma^t
\mathbf{r}_t
\right]
=
\left[
J^{\mathrm{sec}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{bus}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{cost}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi)
\right]^\top .
\label{eq:return-vector}
\end{equation}
All four objectives are written in maximization form. In particular,
\(J^{\mathrm{cost}}\) is a utility-transformed Defense Cost objective,
where larger values correspond to lower realized response burden.

Security Effectiveness captures general containment of attacker presence
and progression across the enterprise environment. Business Continuity
captures preservation of ordinary service operation under defensive
interventions. Defense Cost captures response burden and reliance on
costly or disruptive actions. Critical-Asset Safety captures tail-risk
behavior around the critical-asset set; it is distinct from Security
Effectiveness because it focuses on first critical hits, persistent
critical compromise, and critical dwell rather than general containment
alone.

Using the critical-breach and pre-critical indicators defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:threat-model}, we define two additional step-level
indicators for Critical-Asset Safety. The first critical-hit indicator is
defined as:
\begin{equation}
f_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1
\;\wedge\;
\sum_{k=0}^{t-1} b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}=0
\right\}.
\label{eq:first-hit}
\end{equation}
For a persistence horizon \(L\), the persistent critical-compromise
indicator is defined as:
\begin{equation}
p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
t\geq L-1
\;\wedge\;
\sum_{k=t-L+1}^{t}
b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
L
\right\}.
\label{eq:persistent-step}
\end{equation}
In evaluation, \(L\) is fixed by the semantic audit protocol.

We define the per-step Critical-Asset Safety reward as:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{pre}} b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{hit}} f_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{dwell}} b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{persist}} p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}},
\label{eq:crit-reward}
\end{equation}
where all coefficients are non-negative. The corresponding policy return
\(J^{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi)\) is the fourth component of
Eq.~\ref{eq:return-vector}. This objective encourages containment in the
pre-critical window before attacker progression becomes realized
critical-asset compromise. Optimizing Critical-Asset Safety does not
provide a formal safety guarantee; it provides a decision objective and
an audit signal for reducing critical-asset risk under the modeled
defense process.

For replay-level diagnostics, we use trajectory summaries such as:
\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}>0
\right\},
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}},
\label{eq:crit-audit-summary}
\end{equation}
which indicate whether a critical breach ever occurs and how long
critical compromise persists. Additional audit summaries, including
first critical-hit time and persistent breach rate, are defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:critical-audit} and reported in the experimental
protocol. These trajectory-level variables are used for semantic audit
and event-style constraints, not as privileged policy inputs.

\subsection{Operational Acceptance Constraints}
\label{subsec:constraints}

Operational preferences are distinct from hard acceptance constraints. A
preference specifies how an operator ranks trade-offs among the four
objectives, whereas a hard constraint specifies behavior that must be
rejected regardless of scalar utility.

Let \(\Pi\) denote a finite archive of candidate defense policies. For a
policy \(\pi\in\Pi\), we define the \(m\)-th operational violation
summary as:
\begin{equation}
g_m(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi}
\left[
c_m(\tau)
\right],
\qquad
m=1,\ldots,M ,
\label{eq:constraint-expectation}
\end{equation}
where \(c_m(\tau)\geq 0\) is a trajectory-level violation function. For
event-style constraints, \(c_m(\tau)\) may be an indicator such as
\(B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\), so that \(g_m(\pi)\) is an event rate under
the modeled process. Other constraints may summarize business-service
disruption, excessive response burden, or high-disruption intervention
patterns.

For operational limits
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}=[\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_M]^\top\), we define the
acceptable archive as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\left\{
\pi\in\Pi:
g_m(\pi)\leq \delta_m,
\; m=1,\ldots,M
\right\}.
\label{eq:acceptable-archive}
\end{equation}
When the archive \(\Pi\) is clear from context, we write
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) for brevity.

We also define the mean constraint violation as:
\begin{equation}
V(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\left[
g_m(\pi)-\delta_m
\right]_+ ,
\label{eq:mean-violation}
\end{equation}
where \([x]_+=\max(x,0)\). A policy with
\(V(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})=0\) satisfies all specified limits. In
experiments, \(g_m(\pi)\) is estimated from a fixed rollout protocol, so
operational acceptance is empirical under the stated protocol rather
than a formal guarantee over all possible executions or attacker
variants.

The acceptance layer is a hard filter, not another preference weight. A
policy can achieve high scalar utility and still be rejected if it
violates operational limits or exhibits unacceptable critical-risk
behavior.

\subsection{Acceptable Pareto Assignment}
\label{subsec:acceptable-pareto-assignment}

Because the four objectives can conflict, a single defense policy need
not be best under all operational regimes. We use Pareto dominance to
describe objective-space trade-offs. For two policies \(\pi_a\) and
\(\pi_b\), we say that \(\pi_a\) objective-dominates \(\pi_b\), written
\(\pi_a \succ_J \pi_b\), if:
\begin{equation}
J_i(\pi_a)
\geq
J_i(\pi_b),
\qquad
\forall i\in
\{\mathrm{sec},\mathrm{bus},\mathrm{cost},\mathrm{crit}\},
\label{eq:dominance-weak}
\end{equation}
and the inequality is strict for at least one objective. Since all
objectives are in maximization form, this dominance relation has a
consistent direction.

We define the raw Pareto set induced by archive \(\Pi\) as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}(\Pi)
=
\left\{
\pi\in\Pi:
\nexists \pi'\in\Pi
\text{ such that }
\pi'\succ_J \pi
\right\}.
\label{eq:raw-pareto-set}
\end{equation}
Raw Pareto optimality is not sufficient for operational selection. A
policy may be non-dominated in objective space but unacceptable if it
violates hard limits on disruption, response burden, or critical-risk
behavior.

We therefore define the acceptable Pareto set after applying the
operational acceptance filter:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\left\{
\pi\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta}):
\nexists \pi'\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})
\text{ such that }
\pi'\succ_J \pi
\right\}.
\label{eq:acceptable-pareto-set}
\end{equation}
This distinction is central to autonomous defense: Pareto dominance
describes objective-space trade-off quality, whereas operational
acceptance determines whether a candidate policy is eligible for
selection at all.

Let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_4,
\qquad
\Delta_4
=
\left\{
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}:
\sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i=1
\right\}
\label{eq:preference-simplex}
\end{equation}
denote an operator preference over the four objectives. Because the
objectives may have different numerical scales, scalar utility is
computed on a calibrated objective vector:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)
=
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{J}(\pi)),
\label{eq:calibrated-objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{C}\) is fixed by the evaluation protocol and is
coordinate-wise monotone.

Given preference \(\mathbf{w}\) and operational limits
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), we define the constrained assignment rule as:
\begin{equation}
\pi^\star(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\arg\max_{\pi\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})}
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
\label{eq:constrained-assignment}
\end{equation}
If
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})=\emptyset\),
the assignment is marked infeasible rather than forcing a policy that
violates hard operational limits. This constrained assignment is the
operational counterpart of policy-set assignment in multi-objective
reinforcement learning: preferences select among acceptable policies,
while hard constraints determine which policies are acceptable in the
first place.


\section{DA-CPSL: Learning Constrained Defense Policy Sets}
\label{sec:learning}

The formulation in Section~\ref{sec:formulation} requires a learning
procedure that constructs a policy archive, not a single scalar-reward
policy. We propose \emph{Defense-Aware Constrained Policy-Set Learning}
(DA-CPSL), which learns a finite archive of defense policies and selects
policies only through the operational acceptance layer defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:acceptable-pareto-assignment}.

DA-CPSL uses a two-stage archive-construction structure inspired by
policy-set MORL, particularly C-MORL~\cite{liu2025cmorl}, which
initializes diverse policies and extends selected policies toward
different objective directions. We use this structure as an archive
construction template, but the learning object and assignment rule are
defense-specific: every candidate is evaluated by objective returns,
operational violations, and semantic critical-risk audits, and final
assignment is performed only over the acceptable Pareto set.

\subsection{Method Overview}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-overview}

DA-CPSL maintains a finite archive of candidate defense policies:
\[
\Pi=\{\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_{K_\Pi}\}.
\]
Each candidate policy is stored as an evaluated record:
\begin{equation}
x(\pi)
=
\left(
\pi,
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi),
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)
\right),
\label{eq:dacpsl-record}
\end{equation}
where \(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\) estimates the four objective returns,
\(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi)\) estimates operational violation summaries,
and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)\) records replay-level semantic
audit variables such as critical breach, persistent critical breach,
first critical-hit time, and critical dwell.

The method has two stages. Stage~1 trains an initial set of
preference-diverse defense policies. Stage~2 expands the archive by
selecting parent policies from sparse and operationally promising
regions of the archive, then extending each parent toward individual
objective directions while preserving other objectives and respecting
operational acceptance constraints. After training, the archive supports
preference-conditioned assignment without retraining. If no candidate
satisfies the specified operational limits, DA-CPSL returns
\emph{infeasible} rather than forcing an unacceptable policy choice.

\paragraph{Workflow.}
DA-CPSL proceeds as follows: (i) train initial policies under sampled
objective preferences; (ii) evaluate every candidate by objective
returns, constraint violations, and semantic audits; (iii) construct raw
and acceptable Pareto views of the archive; (iv) extend selected
candidates through acceptance-aware Pareto extension; and (v) perform
operational assignment using Eq.~\ref{eq:constrained-assignment}.

\subsection{Stage 1: Preference-Diverse Initialization}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-stage1}

Stage~1 constructs an initial archive by training defense policies under
sampled objective preferences. Let
\[
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\subset\Delta_4
\]
denote the initial preference set over Security Effectiveness, Business
Continuity, Defense Cost, and Critical-Asset Safety. For each
\(\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\), DA-CPSL trains a policy by
maximizing the scalarized return:
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathbf{w}}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\gamma^t
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_t
\right].
\label{eq:stage1-scalarized-return}
\end{equation}

The preference vector is used only to induce diversity during
initialization. Once a policy is trained, it is stored as a
preference-independent candidate and can later be evaluated under any
operator preference. After training, each initial policy is evaluated
under the fixed evaluation protocol to estimate
\(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\), \(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi)\), and
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)\). The result is an initial evaluated
archive \(\Pi_{\mathrm{init}}\).

This initialization stage provides diverse starting points, but it does
not by itself guarantee operational acceptability. A policy trained under
a particular preference may still violate business-disruption, response
burden, or critical-risk limits. Therefore, every policy must pass the
acceptance filter before it can be used for operational assignment.

\subsection{Stage 2: Acceptance-Aware Pareto Extension}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-stage2}

Stage~2 expands the archive by improving selected parent policies along
individual objective directions. Let
\[
\mathcal{I}
=
\{
\mathrm{sec},
\mathrm{bus},
\mathrm{cost},
\mathrm{crit}
\}
\]
denote the objective index set. Given a parent policy \(\pi_r\) and a
target objective \(\ell\in\mathcal{I}\), the extension step seeks a child
policy that improves objective \(\ell\) while avoiding unacceptable
regression on the other objectives and satisfying operational acceptance
constraints.

\paragraph{Parent selection.}
At each extension round, DA-CPSL computes archive views using the
definitions in Section~\ref{subsec:acceptable-pareto-assignment}. When
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\) is
non-empty, parent policies are selected from this acceptable Pareto set.
If no acceptable Pareto candidate exists early in training, the method
may use the raw Pareto view for exploration, but such candidates remain
ineligible for operational assignment unless they later pass the
acceptance filter.

Within the candidate source set, DA-CPSL encourages objective-space
coverage by prioritizing sparse regions of the calibrated objective
space. Extreme candidates for each objective are retained when possible,
and remaining parent slots are assigned to candidates that improve
frontier coverage while exhibiting lower constraint violation and lower
critical-risk audit summaries. Thus, parent selection treats diversity
and operational acceptability as separate requirements.

\paragraph{Extension objective.}
Let
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)
=
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{J}(\pi))
\]
be the calibrated objective vector from
Eq.~\ref{eq:calibrated-objective}. For \(\beta\in(0,1]\), we define the
parent-relative preservation threshold as:
\begin{equation}
d_i(\pi_r;\beta)
=
\beta\,
\widetilde{J}_i(\pi_r),
\qquad i\in\mathcal{I},\; i\neq \ell .
\label{eq:parent-relative-threshold}
\end{equation}
The idealized extension problem is defined as:
\begin{align}
\pi_{\ell}^{+}
\in
\arg\max_{\pi}
&\quad
\widetilde{J}_{\ell}(\pi)
\label{eq:ideal-extension}
\\
\mathrm{s.t.}
&\quad
\widetilde{J}_{i}(\pi)
\geq
d_i(\pi_r;\beta),
\qquad i\in\mathcal{I},\; i\neq \ell ,
\nonumber\\
&\quad
g_m(\pi)
\leq
\delta_m,
\qquad m=1,\ldots,M .
\nonumber
\end{align}
The first constraint family prevents extension toward one objective from
destroying the other objectives. The second constraint family enforces
operational acceptance limits.

In implementation, Eq.~\ref{eq:ideal-extension} is optimized using a
policy-gradient surrogate with return-preservation terms and violation
penalties. The surrogate is a training mechanism, not the final
acceptance decision. After each extension update, the child policy is
re-evaluated under the fixed evaluation protocol to obtain
\(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}\), \(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\), and
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\). Operational eligibility is determined
from these post-update evaluations. Appendix~\ref{app:dacpsl-details}
provides the detailed surrogate and algorithmic workflow.

\subsection{Archive Update and Semantic Acceptance}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-archive-update}

A child policy is not considered acceptable merely because it improves a
target objective or increases raw archive quality. DA-CPSL updates the
archive using evaluated candidate records and separates three views of
the archive.

\paragraph{Raw archive.}
The raw archive stores evaluated candidates and their records
\(x(\pi)\). It supports analysis of candidate-set quality and retains
policies that may be useful for later extension, even if they are not
acceptable under the current operational limits.

\paragraph{Acceptable archive.}
The acceptable archive
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\) contains candidates that
satisfy the operational acceptance constraints in
Eq.~\ref{eq:acceptable-archive}. A candidate that violates these limits
may remain in the raw archive, but it is not eligible for operational
assignment under the corresponding \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\).

\paragraph{Semantic audit.}
The audit vector \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)\) records
security-relevant trajectory behavior. DA-CPSL uses these summaries to
identify candidates that appear strong in average return but exhibit
unacceptable critical-risk behavior, such as realized critical breach,
persistent critical breach, excessive critical dwell, or severe near-miss
behavior in the pre-critical window.

The archive update rule can be summarized as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi
\leftarrow
\Pi
\cup
\{
\pi_{\ell}^{+}
\},
\qquad
x(\pi_{\ell}^{+})
=
\left(
\pi_{\ell}^{+},
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi_{\ell}^{+}),
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi_{\ell}^{+}),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi_{\ell}^{+})
\right).
\label{eq:archive-update}
\end{equation}
The raw and acceptable Pareto sets are then computed as views of this
evaluated archive. This design avoids prematurely deleting a raw
dominated policy that may remain useful if the policies dominating it
fail operational acceptance constraints.

\subsection{Operational Assignment and Complexity}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-assignment}

After archive construction, operational adaptation is performed by
assignment rather than retraining. Given an operator preference
\(\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_4\) and operational limits
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), DA-CPSL computes the acceptable Pareto set
\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})
\]
and applies the constrained assignment rule in
Eq.~\ref{eq:constrained-assignment}. If the acceptable Pareto set is
empty, DA-CPSL reports the assignment as infeasible.

This behavior is deliberate. A high-utility policy that violates hard
operational limits is not treated as an acceptable substitute. For
diagnostics, the system may report the least-violating candidate, but it
is not counted as a successful operational assignment.

Let \(n=|\mathcal{I}|\), \(K_{\mathrm{ext}}\) be the number of extension
rounds, and \(N_{\mathrm{parent}}\) be the number of parent policies
selected per round. Under a fixed per-extension training budget, the
number of extension optimization calls scales as:
\begin{equation}
O(n K_{\mathrm{ext}} N_{\mathrm{parent}}).
\label{eq:dacpsl-complexity}
\end{equation}
The additional cost relative to generic policy-set learning comes from
candidate evaluation, operational constraint estimation, and semantic
audit. These costs are necessary for distinguishing high-return policies
from operationally acceptable defense policies.

DA-CPSL therefore produces not only a set of high-return policies, but
also an evaluated archive whose candidates can be filtered by hard
operational limits, audited for critical-risk behavior, and assigned
under changing operator preferences.


\section{Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation-setup}

This section describes how we instantiate the threat model and
formulation in a controlled cyber-range evaluation. The goal of the
evaluation is not to show that an autonomous defender is ready for
unsupervised production operation. Instead, we test whether DA-CPSL
constructs a stronger defense policy archive, whether its assignments
satisfy shared operational limits, and whether the selected policies
exhibit safer replay-level critical-asset semantics under the evaluated
protocol.

\subsection{Evaluation Questions}
\label{subsec:eval-questions}

We organize the evaluation around four research questions.

\paragraph{RQ1: Archive quality.}
Does DA-CPSL construct a stronger four-objective defense policy archive
than scalarized or non-acceptance-aware alternatives? This question
evaluates objective-space candidate quality using policy-set metrics
such as hypervolume, expected utility, coverage, and the number of
distinct assigned policies.

\paragraph{RQ2: Operational acceptability.}
Does DA-CPSL produce more acceptable policy assignments under shared
operational limits? This question evaluates feasible-assignment rate,
mean constraint violation, and the realized security, business,
response-burden, and critical-asset outcomes of assigned policies.

\paragraph{RQ3: Replay-level cyber semantics.}
Do selected DA-CPSL policies contain attacker progression before it
crosses from pre-critical exposure into realized critical breach? This
question evaluates trajectory-level semantic audit metrics, including
ever-critical-breach rate, persistent-critical-breach rate, critical
dwell, first critical-hit time, and pre-critical response behavior.

\paragraph{RQ4: Component necessity.}
Which components are necessary for the observed behavior? This question
tests whether Stage~2 extension, acceptance-aware shaping, and
Critical-Asset Safety are required for both archive quality and
operational acceptability.

\subsection{Cyber-Range and Objective Instantiation}
\label{subsec:eval-cyber-range}

We instantiate the threat model in CybORG++ Scenario2, a controlled
enterprise cyber range for autonomous defense. The instantiation provides
partial observations, host-level response actions, post-compromise
attacker progression, business-impacting defensive side effects, and
critical-asset compromise semantics. The cyber range is used as an
experimental realization of the problem class in
Section~\ref{sec:problem-threat}; it is not the source of the threat
model itself.

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments use the standard fixed baseline
attacker process, a finite horizon of \(T=100\), vector observations, and
the same Scenario2 configuration. The fixed attacker process is a
deliberate evaluation choice: it lets us compare defense learning methods
under the same attack-defense process. Robustness to online adaptive
attackers is outside the main evaluation scope.

The four objectives in the cyber range correspond to the formulation in
Section~\ref{subsec:objectives}: Security Effectiveness, Business
Continuity, Defense Cost, and Critical-Asset Safety. Critical-Asset
Safety is optimized as the fourth objective and is also audited through
semantic replay summaries. The semantic audit is not a fifth optimization
objective; it is used to diagnose whether a policy with strong average
return nevertheless exhibits unacceptable critical-risk behavior.

\subsection{Compared Methods}
\label{subsec:eval-methods}

We compare methods that are evaluated under the same four-objective
protocol and shared assignment thresholds.

\paragraph{DA-CPSL.}
This is the full method. It uses the Stage~1 archive and performs
acceptance-aware Stage~2 extension with Critical-Asset Safety enabled.
The final policy assignment is performed from the acceptable Pareto set.

\paragraph{Stage-1 Archive.}
This ablation evaluates the preference-diverse initialization stage
without Stage~2 extension. It tests whether the initial policy archive is
sufficient to support acceptable assignment.

\paragraph{Weighted-Sum PPO.}
This scalarized baseline trains independent policies under fixed explicit
preference vectors over the four objectives. It tests whether separately
trained scalarized policies can substitute for an evaluated policy
archive.

\paragraph{Preference-Conditioned PPO.}
This baseline trains a single policy conditioned on the preference
vector. It tests whether direct preference conditioning can cover the
four-objective trade-off surface without maintaining a separate evaluated
policy archive.

\paragraph{Lagrangian PPO.}
This constrained single-policy baseline augments scalarized training with
Lagrangian penalties for operational constraint violations. It tests
whether a single constrained policy can satisfy shared operational limits
under heterogeneous preferences.

\paragraph{Unconstrained Stage~2.}
This ablation uses the same Stage~2 archive-extension structure but
removes acceptance-aware constraint shaping. It tests whether raw Pareto
extension alone is sufficient for operationally acceptable defense.

We do not mix older baselines that were not rerun under the unified
four-objective protocol into the main tables. Such comparisons would
conflate differences in objectives, thresholds, and semantic-audit
protocols.

\subsection{Metrics and Assignment Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval-metrics}

The metrics follow the three-layer structure of
Section~\ref{sec:formulation}: objectives, constraints, and audits.

\paragraph{Objective-space archive metrics.}
To evaluate candidate-set quality, we report hypervolume, expected
utility, coverage ratio, the number of distinct assigned policies, and
the number of retained Pareto records. Hypervolume and expected utility
are standard policy-set MORL metrics for evaluating Pareto-front quality
and preference-conditioned utility; in our setting, they measure
objective-space quality but not operational acceptability.

\paragraph{Operational assignment metrics.}
For each method, we use the same preference grid and shared operational
thresholds. An assignment is acceptable only if the selected policy
satisfies the operational limits defined by the evaluation protocol. We
report feasible-assignment rate, mean constraint violation, assigned
objective returns, final critical compromised hosts, and critical-impact
count. If no acceptable policy exists for a preference, the assignment is
marked infeasible rather than replaced by a high-utility but
unacceptable policy.

\paragraph{Replay-level semantic audit metrics.}
For selected policies, we perform replay-level semantic audit using
\(20\) replay episodes per selected policy. The audit reports
ever-critical-breach rate, persistent-critical-breach rate, critical
dwell, first critical-hit time, near-miss tiers, and phase-conditioned
response behavior. Near-miss statistics are diagnostic: a policy can be
breach-free under the evaluated protocol while still showing
pre-critical exposure. We therefore interpret zero observed critical
breach together with near-miss behavior as evidence of pre-critical
containment, not as evidence of risk elimination.

\paragraph{Assignment protocol.}
For archive-quality evaluation, each candidate policy is evaluated over
the preference grid with the same preference step. For operational
assignment, each method uses the same constrained assignment rule from
Eq.~\ref{eq:constrained-assignment}. For semantic audit, we replay the
selected policy under the fixed evaluation protocol and report
trajectory-level cyber semantics. All reported acceptance results are
empirical under this protocol.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:eval-reproducibility}

All main experiments are run with seeds \(7\), \(11\), and \(19\). The
Stage~2 archive is initialized from the corresponding Stage~1 archive
for the same seed. Candidate-set evaluation, operational assignment, and
semantic audit use the same seed set and the same cyber-range
instantiation.

The implementation stores each candidate as an evaluated record
containing objective estimates, operational violation estimates, and
semantic audit summaries. The artifact package includes the configuration
files for Stage~1, Stage~2, archive-quality evaluation, operational
assignment, table export, and semantic audit. Additional hyperparameters,
semantic rule definitions, per-seed outputs, and full audit vectors are
reported in the appendix.

acceptance-aware shaping reduces feasible-assignment rate and increases
mean violation. It also increases questionable post-foothold behavior in
the semantic audit. This shows that Pareto extension alone is not enough:
extension must be constrained by operational and critical-risk
acceptance criteria.

\paragraph{Critical-Asset Safety solves a representation problem.}
Panel C compares the four-objective formulation with a three-objective
variant that lacks Critical-Asset Safety. The three-objective variant can
appear competitive in conventional objective-space terms, but it fails
under the critical-risk semantics required by the current threat model:
its selected policies exhibit high ever-critical-breach and persistent
critical-breach rates, whereas the four-objective formulation keeps these
indicators near zero under the evaluated protocol. This demonstrates that
Critical-Asset Safety is not a cosmetic auxiliary term; it changes the
representation of acceptable defense trade-offs.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{subsec:results-summary}

The evaluation supports four findings. First, DA-CPSL constructs a
stronger four-objective defense policy archive than scalarized and
non-acceptance-aware alternatives. Second, the archive supports more
acceptable operational assignments under shared hard limits. Third,
semantic replay audit shows that the selected DA-CPSL policy changes the
post-foothold response process, reducing drift and low-value action loops
while keeping observed critical breach at zero under the protocol.
Fourth, the ablation study shows that Stage~2 extension, acceptance-aware
shaping, and Critical-Asset Safety each address a distinct failure mode:
discovery, acceptability, and representation.

Taken together, the results support the following bounded claim:
DA-CPSL provides stronger pre-critical containment under the evaluated
threat model and cyber-range protocol. They do not imply formal safety,
readiness for unsupervised production operation, or elimination of all
cyber risk.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Defense and Cyber Ranges}

Autonomous cyber defense has emerged as a research direction for using
learning agents to detect, contain, and respond to attacker behavior in
networked environments. Surveys of automated and reinforcement-learning
based cyber defense emphasize that the field requires both realistic
cyber environments and decision-making agents capable of operating under
partial observability, large action spaces, and adversarial dynamics
~\cite{vyas2023automated,palmer2023drlsurvey}. CybORG introduced a gym
for autonomous cyber agents, and subsequent work has used CybORG and
CAGE-style challenge settings to study learned network defenders
~\cite{standen2021cyborg,foley2022autonomous}. CybORG++ extends this
line with improved tooling and faster experimentation for enterprise
network defense research~\cite{emerson2024cyborgpp}.

Most cyber-range ACD work evaluates a trained defender by episodic
reward, action success, or scenario-specific measures. Foundational
studies have examined tabular learning and deep RL defenders in cyber
ranges~\cite{applebaum2022bridging,foley2022autonomous}, while reward
shaping work has shown that the training signal strongly affects learned
defense behavior~\cite{bates2023reward}. Our work uses the same general
cyber-range tradition but addresses a different decision problem. Rather
than training one defense policy for a single scalar reward, DA-CPSL
learns an evaluated archive of defense policies and performs constrained
assignment under operational preferences and hard limits.

\subsection{Defensive Trade-offs and Operational Constraints}

Cyber defense actions often have operational side effects. Restoring a
host may remove attacker presence but disrupt services; deception may
slow attacker progression but consume response resources; conservative
response may preserve short-term continuity while increasing exposure
around critical assets. This motivates treating autonomous defense as a
decision problem with competing objectives rather than as pure attack
suppression. In critical-service or IT/OT-adjacent environments, this
trade-off is particularly important because OT security guidance
emphasizes safety, reliability, and continuity alongside cybersecurity
risk reduction~\cite{nist_sp80082r3}.

Existing ACD and cyber-range studies recognize operationally meaningful
metrics, but they usually do not formalize the defender's decision object
as a policy archive with hard acceptance constraints. Our formulation
separates preferences from hard limits: preferences rank acceptable
trade-offs, while operational constraints reject unacceptable behavior
regardless of scalar utility. This distinction is central to DA-CPSL and
to our evaluation, where objective-space quality, operational
assignment, and replay-level semantic audit are reported separately.

\subsection{Safe, Constrained, and Assured Learning}

Constrained reinforcement learning studies policies that maximize return
while satisfying expected cost or safety constraints. Constrained MDPs
provide the classical formulation~\cite{altman2021cmdp}, and policy
optimization methods such as CPO and interior-point policy optimization
provide algorithmic mechanisms for constraint-aware learning
~\cite{achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo}. These methods are closely related to
our Stage~2 extension problem, where a policy improves one objective
while preserving other objective values and satisfying operational
limits.

Our setting differs from conventional constrained RL in two ways. First,
we do not train a single policy for one constrained objective. DA-CPSL
constructs a policy archive so that different operator preferences can be
handled by assignment rather than retraining. Second, our constraints
include replay-level critical-risk semantics and empirical operational
acceptance. We therefore do not claim formal safety guarantees. Instead,
we treat acceptance as an empirical filter under the stated evaluation
protocol and report semantic audit evidence to expose critical-risk
behavior that may be hidden by average return.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning and Policy-Set Methods}

Multi-objective reinforcement learning addresses settings where multiple
objectives must be optimized jointly and decision makers may have
different preferences over those objectives~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022practical}.
One common approach trains a single preference-conditioned policy that
takes the preference vector as input~\cite{yang2019generalized}. Another
approach trains or maintains a finite set of policies to approximate a
Pareto set, enabling later policy assignment for a given preference
~\cite{vanmoffaert2014sets,xu2020prediction,alegre2022optimistic,felten2024morld}.
Policy-set methods are attractive for autonomous defense because they
allow the defender to adapt to changing operational preferences without
retraining a new policy for every preference.

C-MORL is particularly relevant because it uses a two-stage policy-set
learning procedure: preference-diverse initialization followed by
Pareto-front extension, where one objective is improved while the others
are constrained~\cite{liu2025cmorl}. It then performs policy assignment
from the learned Pareto set. DA-CPSL adopts this policy-set learning
philosophy, but changes the problem object and assignment rule for
autonomous defense. Each candidate policy is evaluated by objective
returns, operational violation summaries, and semantic audit variables;
final assignment is performed from the acceptable Pareto set; and the
assignment may return infeasible when no candidate satisfies the hard
limits. Thus, DA-CPSL is not only a MORL archive-construction method
applied to a cyber range. It is a defense-aware, acceptance-aware, and
audit-aware policy-set learning framework for autonomous cyber defense
under operational constraints.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studied autonomous cyber defense as a constrained
multi-objective policy-archive problem rather than a single
scalar-reward policy-learning problem. We argued that operationally
meaningful defense requires separating three concerns that are often
collapsed in reinforcement-learning formulations: objective trade-offs,
hard acceptance constraints, and preference-conditioned policy
assignment. In particular, we introduced Critical-Asset Safety as a
first-class objective to capture tail-risk behavior around critical
assets, while keeping replay-level semantic audit as an explicit
diagnostic mechanism.

We proposed DA-CPSL, a defense-aware constrained policy-set learning
method that constructs an evaluated archive of candidate defense
policies. DA-CPSL combines preference-diverse initialization,
acceptance-aware Pareto extension, semantic candidate auditing, and
constrained assignment over the acceptable Pareto set. This design allows
operator preferences to select among acceptable policies while preventing
preference weights from overriding hard operational limits. If no
candidate satisfies the specified limits, DA-CPSL reports the assignment
as infeasible rather than forcing an unacceptable policy choice.

Our controlled cyber-range evaluation shows that DA-CPSL improves both
objective-space archive quality and operational assignment
acceptability. Across the evaluated seeds, DA-CPSL achieves stronger
hypervolume and expected utility than scalarized and non-acceptance-aware
alternatives, increases the feasible-assignment rate, and substantially
reduces mean constraint violation. Replay-level semantic audit further
shows no observed critical breach or persistent critical breach for the
selected DA-CPSL policy under the fixed evaluation protocol, while
near-miss trajectories remain visible. This supports a bounded
interpretation: DA-CPSL improves pre-critical containment under the
evaluated threat model and cyber-range protocol, but it does not
eliminate cyber risk.

The results also clarify the role of the main components. Stage~2
extension improves archive discovery; acceptance-aware shaping improves
operational acceptability; and Critical-Asset Safety changes the
representation of acceptable defense trade-offs. These findings suggest
that autonomous defense should be evaluated not only by average return or
raw Pareto quality, but also by whether selected policies satisfy
operational limits and exhibit acceptable replay-level security
semantics.

Several limitations remain. Our evaluation uses a controlled cyber-range
instantiation with a fixed attacker process for fair comparison across
methods. The reported acceptance and breach-free results are empirical
under this protocol and should not be interpreted as formal safety
guarantees, readiness for unsupervised production operation, or
robustness to all attacker variants. Future work should extend
DA-CPSL-style policy archives to broader attacker variation, richer
incident-response workflows, human approval and escalation processes,
and complementary formal or runtime assurance mechanisms for learned
defense policies.

\section{Additional Formal Details}
\label{app:formal-details}

This appendix provides formal details that support the main formulation
and method. The main paper keeps only the definitions required for the
core argument; this appendix gives the complete audit variables,
empirical acceptance estimates, DA-CPSL workflow, extension surrogate,
and the relationship to C-MORL.

\subsection{Critical-Risk Audit Variables}
\label{app:critical-audit}

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) denote the set of cyber entities and
\(\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) denote the critical-asset set. For
an audit trajectory, let \(C_t\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) be the set of
compromised entities at time \(t\). As in the main text, we define the
critical-breach indicator as:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbb{1}\{C_t\cap\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset\}.
\label{eq:app-critical-breach}
\end{equation}
We define the enterprise-foothold indicator as:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{foot}}
=
\mathbb{1}\{C_t\setminus\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset\}.
\label{eq:app-enterprise-foothold}
\end{equation}
We define the pre-critical exposure indicator as:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
C_t\cap\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
\;\wedge\;
\rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})>0
\right\},
\label{eq:app-precritical}
\end{equation}
where \(\rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})\) is a policy-independent audit function
that measures elevated exposure around critical assets.

The first critical-hit indicator is defined as:
\begin{equation}
f_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1
\;\wedge\;
\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}
b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}=0
\right\}.
\label{eq:app-first-hit-indicator}
\end{equation}
For a persistence horizon \(L\), the step-level persistent critical
compromise indicator is defined as:
\begin{equation}
p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
t\geq L-1
\;\wedge\;
\sum_{k=t-L+1}^{t}
b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
L
\right\}.
\label{eq:app-persistent-step}
\end{equation}
The special case \(L=2\) corresponds to critical compromise persisting
for two consecutive timesteps.

For a trajectory \(\tau\), we define the event-level critical breach
indicator as:
\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}>0
\right\}.
\label{eq:app-ever-critical}
\end{equation}
We define critical dwell as:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}.
\label{eq:app-critical-dwell}
\end{equation}
We define the first critical-hit time as:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\min\{t:b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1\},
\qquad
T_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)=\infty
\text{ if no critical breach occurs}.
\label{eq:app-first-critical-time}
\end{equation}
We define the trajectory-level persistent critical breach indicator as:
\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{persist}}^{(L)}(\tau)
=
\mathbb{1}
\left\{
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}}>0
\right\}.
\label{eq:app-persistent-trajectory}
\end{equation}

These trajectory-level variables are used for replay-level semantic
audit, event-style operational constraints, and diagnostic reporting. They
are not privileged inputs to the defense policy.

\subsection{Empirical Operational Acceptance}
\label{app:empirical-acceptance}

The population-level operational violation summary is defined in
Eq.~\ref{eq:constraint-expectation}. In experiments, it is estimated from
a fixed rollout protocol. For \(N\) evaluation trajectories
\(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_N\), we estimate \(g_m(\pi)\) as:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{g}_m(\pi)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{j=1}^{N}
c_m(\tau_j),
\qquad
\tau_j\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi .
\label{eq:app-empirical-g}
\end{equation}
The corresponding empirical mean violation is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{V}(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\left[
\widehat{g}_m(\pi)-\delta_m
\right]_+ .
\label{eq:app-empirical-violation}
\end{equation}

When \(c_m(\tau)\in[0,1]\), a standard Hoeffding bound gives the
following one-sided confidence statement. For any \(\alpha\in(0,1)\),
with probability at least \(1-\alpha\):
\begin{equation}
g_m(\pi)
\leq
\widehat{g}_m(\pi)
+
\sqrt{
\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2N}
}.
\label{eq:app-hoeffding}
\end{equation}
For event-style constraints with zero observed events,
\(\widehat{g}_m(\pi)=0\), this bound becomes:
\begin{equation}
g_m(\pi)
\leq
\sqrt{
\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2N}
}
\qquad
\text{with probability at least }1-\alpha .
\label{eq:app-zero-event-bound}
\end{equation}
Thus, observing no critical breach under a finite evaluation protocol is
evidence of low event rate under that protocol, but it is not a formal
guarantee of zero risk across all executions or attacker variants.

\subsection{Candidate Records and Archive Views}
\label{app:candidate-records}

DA-CPSL stores each evaluated candidate as:
\begin{equation}
x(\pi)
=
\left(
\pi,
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi),
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)
\right).
\label{eq:app-candidate-record}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\) estimates the optimized objective
returns, \(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi)\) estimates operational violation
summaries, and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)\) records semantic
audit variables such as \(B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\),
\(B_{\mathrm{persist}}^{(L)}(\tau)\), \(T_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\), and
\(D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\).

The raw archive is the set of all evaluated candidates. The acceptable
archive for operational limits \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is defined as in
Eq.~\ref{eq:acceptable-archive}. The raw Pareto set and acceptable
Pareto set are different views of the same evaluated archive. This
distinction matters because a raw dominated policy may remain useful when
the policies dominating it fail operational acceptance constraints.

\section{DA-CPSL Algorithmic Details}
\label{app:dacpsl-details}

\subsection{Parent Selection}
\label{app:parent-selection}

At each extension round, DA-CPSL selects parent policies from an archive
view. If the acceptable Pareto set is non-empty, the parent source set is
defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}
=
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta}).
\label{eq:app-parent-source-acceptable}
\end{equation}
If no acceptable Pareto candidate exists early in training, the parent
source set may fall back to the raw Pareto set:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}
=
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}(\Pi).
\label{eq:app-parent-source-raw}
\end{equation}
Candidates selected from the raw Pareto set remain ineligible for
operational assignment unless they later pass the acceptance filter.

To encourage objective-space coverage, we compute crowd-distance-style
diversity scores over calibrated objective vectors. Let
\(\widetilde{J}_i(\pi)\) be the calibrated value of objective \(i\).
For each objective \(i\), sort candidates in \(\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}\) by
\(\widetilde{J}_i\). For an interior candidate \(\pi\), let
\(\pi_i^{-}\) and \(\pi_i^{+}\) denote its immediate neighbors in the
sorted order for objective \(i\). We define the diversity score as:
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{crowd}}(\pi)
=
\sum_i
\frac{
\widetilde{J}_i(\pi_i^{+})
-
\widetilde{J}_i(\pi_i^{-})
}{
\max_{\pi'\in\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}}\widetilde{J}_i(\pi')
-
\min_{\pi'\in\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}}\widetilde{J}_i(\pi')
+
\epsilon
}.
\label{eq:app-crowd-distance}
\end{equation}
Extreme candidates for each objective are retained when possible. Among
remaining candidates, DA-CPSL prioritizes high diversity, low mean
constraint violation, and lower semantic critical-risk summaries.

\subsection{Extension Surrogate}
\label{app:extension-surrogate}

The main text defines the idealized extension problem in
Eq.~\ref{eq:ideal-extension}. In a PPO-style implementation, we optimize
a sampled surrogate that combines target-objective improvement,
return-preservation terms, and violation penalties. Let \(\pi_\theta\)
denote the child policy parameterized by \(\theta\). We write the
extension surrogate as:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\theta;\pi_r,\ell)
=
&\;
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{\ell}(\theta)
+
\frac{1}{t_J}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I},\, i\neq \ell}
\log
\left(
\widehat{\widetilde{J}}_{i}(\pi_\theta)
-
d_i(\pi_r;\beta)
+
\epsilon
\right)
\label{eq:app-extension-surrogate}
\\
&\;
-
\lambda_g
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\left[
\widehat{q}_m(\pi_\theta)
-
\delta_m
\right]_+
-
\lambda_{\psi}
\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi_\theta).
\nonumber
\end{align}
Here, \(\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{\ell}(\theta)\) is a PPO-style
clipped surrogate for the target objective \(\ell\), \(t_J>0\) is the
return-preservation barrier parameter, \(\epsilon>0\) is a numerical
stabilizer, \(\widehat{q}_m\) denotes a training proxy for operational
violations, and \(\widehat{\Psi}_{\mathrm{crit}}\) denotes a
critical-risk penalty derived from semantic diagnostics.

The surrogate in Eq.~\ref{eq:app-extension-surrogate} is a training
mechanism. It is not the final acceptance decision. After the update,
each child policy is re-evaluated under the fixed evaluation protocol to
obtain \(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}\), \(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\), and
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\). Operational eligibility is determined
from these post-update estimates.

\subsection{Full Algorithm}
\label{app:full-algorithm}

\noindent
\textbf{Algorithm A1: DA-CPSL}

\noindent
\textbf{Input:}
initial preference set \(\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\);
objective index set \(\mathcal{I}\);
operational limits \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\);
parent budget \(N_{\mathrm{parent}}\);
extension rounds \(K_{\mathrm{ext}}\);
return-preservation parameter \(\beta\);
evaluation protocol \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}\).

\noindent
\textbf{Output:}
evaluated policy archive \(\Pi\) and constrained assignment rule.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Initialize the evaluated archive as \(\Pi\leftarrow\emptyset\).

\item \textbf{Stage 1: preference-diverse initialization.}
For each \(\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Train a defense policy \(\pi_{\mathbf{w}}\) using the scalarized
return in Eq.~\ref{eq:stage1-scalarized-return}.
\item Evaluate \(\pi_{\mathbf{w}}\) under
\(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}\) to obtain
\[
x(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})
=
\left(
\pi_{\mathbf{w}},
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi_{\mathbf{w}}),
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi_{\mathbf{w}}),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})
\right).
\]
\item Add \(x(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})\) to \(\Pi\).
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Stage 2: acceptance-aware Pareto extension.}
For extension round \(k=1,\ldots,K_{\mathrm{ext}}\):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the raw Pareto set \(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}(\Pi)\) and the
acceptable Pareto set
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\).
\item Select up to \(N_{\mathrm{parent}}\) parent policies using the
parent-selection rule in Appendix~\ref{app:parent-selection}.
\item For each selected parent \(\pi_r\) and each target objective
\(\ell\in\mathcal{I}\):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Train a child policy by optimizing the idealized problem in
Eq.~\ref{eq:ideal-extension}, or its PPO-style surrogate in
Eq.~\ref{eq:app-extension-surrogate}.
\item Evaluate the child under \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{eval}}\) to obtain
\(x(\pi_{\ell}^{+})\).
\item Add the evaluated child record to \(\Pi\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Operational assignment.}
For a given preference and limit pair
\((\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})\), compute
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\). If the set
is empty, return \emph{infeasible}; otherwise return
\[
\pi^\star(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\arg\max_{\pi\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})}
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
\]
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Properties of the Idealized Formulation}
\label{app:theoretical-properties}

This appendix states basic properties of the idealized formulation. These
properties concern the mathematical objects defined in the paper; they
are not formal guarantees of production-network safety.

\paragraph{Proposition A.1: Monotone calibration preserves dominance.}
Suppose \(\mathcal{C}\) is coordinate-wise monotone. If
\(\pi_a\succ_J \pi_b\), then
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{J}(\pi_a))\) weakly dominates
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{J}(\pi_b))\), with strict improvement in at least
one objective whenever \(\mathcal{C}\) is strictly monotone on that
coordinate.

\emph{Proof sketch.}
Pareto dominance requires \(J_i(\pi_a)\geq J_i(\pi_b)\) for every
objective \(i\), with strict inequality for at least one objective.
Coordinate-wise monotonicity of \(\mathcal{C}\) preserves the weak
inequality in every coordinate. If \(\mathcal{C}\) is strictly monotone
on a coordinate where the original inequality is strict, the calibrated
vector also has a strict improvement on that coordinate.

\paragraph{Proposition A.2: Assignment optimality within the acceptable Pareto set.}
If
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\neq\emptyset\),
then the assignment rule in Eq.~\ref{eq:constrained-assignment} returns a
policy that maximizes calibrated linear utility over the acceptable
Pareto set.

\emph{Proof sketch.}
The result follows directly from the definition of
\(\pi^\star(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})\) as the maximizer of
\(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\) over
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\). If the
set is empty, the method returns infeasible by definition.

\paragraph{Proposition A.3: Acceptance filtering enforces the specified constraints.}
For any policy \(\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\),
we have \(g_m(\pi)\leq\delta_m\) for all \(m=1,\ldots,M\).

\emph{Proof sketch.}
This follows immediately from the definition of
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{acc}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\) in
Eq.~\ref{eq:acceptable-archive}. The empirical version holds with
\(\widehat{g}_m\) replacing \(g_m\) under the evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Objective Signal Mapping}
\label{app:objective-signal-mapping}

The four objectives in the main text are implemented through
rollout-level and step-level cyber-defense signals. Security
Effectiveness captures attacker suppression, recovery from compromise,
and reduction of security exposure. Business Continuity captures
business-service preservation and disruption induced by defensive
interventions. Defense Cost captures response burden, including reliance
on costly or high-disruption actions. Critical-Asset Safety captures
critical-risk behavior, including critical breach, persistent critical
compromise, first critical-hit time, and critical dwell.

The exact coefficients and signal-extraction rules are implementation
specific and are released with the artifact package. The paper-level
mapping is intentionally semantic rather than coefficient-specific: the
same objective definitions remain valid across small coefficient
recalibrations, while the artifact exposes the exact configuration used
to generate the reported tables and figures.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{app:evaluation-protocol}

All methods are evaluated under the same four-objective protocol, the
same preference grid, the same shared operational thresholds, and the
same constrained assignment rule. Policy-set methods are evaluated both
at the archive level and at the operational-assignment level.

Selected policies are additionally replayed through the semantic audit.
The audit partitions trajectories into enterprise-foothold,
pre-critical, and critical-risk phases before computing risk tiers,
questionable-response rules, and pre-critical action-family statistics.
This separation is important because two policies can share the same
end-state breach label while exhibiting substantially different
post-foothold response behavior.

\subsection{Semantic Audit Rule Families}
\label{app:semantic-rules}

The semantic audit reports two types of trajectory-level evidence. The
first type is hard critical-risk evidence, including whether a replay
ever reaches critical breach, whether critical compromise persists, and
how many steps critical compromise lasts. The second type is
process-level evidence, including questionable response behavior after
enterprise foothold and action-family patterns in the pre-critical
window.

We use the following shorthand in the appendix tables. \emph{Ever}
denotes whether a critical breach occurs at any point in the replay.
\emph{Persist} denotes persistent critical breach. \emph{Dwell} denotes
critical dwell time. \emph{High-Conf.} denotes high-confidence
environment-run risk. \emph{Tier~0 Safe}, \emph{Tier~1 Near-Miss},
\emph{Tier~2 Transient}, and \emph{Tier~3 Persistent} denote the
semantic risk-tier distribution. \emph{Q2}--\emph{Q5} denote
questionable-response rules used to diagnose whether a policy's
post-foothold response remains operationally disciplined. The
pre-critical action summaries report whether response behavior in the
pre-critical window is dominated by restoration, decoy placement, or
critical-path-focused targeting.

\section{Artifact and Reproducibility Notes}
\label{app:artifact-notes}

The artifact package should include the training configurations,
evaluation configurations, semantic audit definitions, table-generation
scripts, figure-generation scripts, and per-seed result files used to
produce the main and appendix results. It should also include the replay
casebook artifacts used for paired semantic analysis.

For double-anonymous review, the artifact should be hosted anonymously
and should not expose author names, institution names, public repository
identities, commit history, or identifying access logs. If any
third-party environment component cannot be mirrored anonymously due to
license or packaging constraints, the artifact should explain the
restriction and provide setup instructions sufficient to reproduce the
methodology and evaluate the reported results.
