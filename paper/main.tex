\begin{abstract}
Autonomous Cyber Defense(ACD) requires sequential decision-making under competing defensive objectives.
Defenders must confront attackers while preserving continuity, limiting cost, and decreasing disruption, making the problem inherently constrained and multi-objective.
However, most existing learning-based approaches optimize a scalarized objective or produce a single deployable policy.
In this paper, we formulate an autonomous defender as a constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning problem with three objectives: security effect, business impact, and defense cost.
We adopt a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework that first-phase learns an initial set of preference-diverse defense policies and then expands the policy set under operational constraints to improve the Pareto-frontier quality and deployment flexibility.
We evaluate both candidate-set quality and final deployment quality in CybORG using Pareto metrics, preference-conditioned utility, and cyber-semantic outcomes.
Experiment results show that our approach yields higher-quality policy sets, broader preference coverage, and stronger feasible deployment choices under strict operational constraints than scalarization-based and conditioned-policy baselines.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

AI-enabled attack--defense interaction is making autonomous cyber defense a time-sensitive sequential decision problem rather than a single-objective control task. In realistic enterprise networks, defenders must respond to attacker progression under operational pressure: actions that suppress compromise may also interrupt critical services, degrade business workflows, or consume limited response capacity. During lateral movement or ransomware-style propagation, this tension becomes especially severe. Aggressive containment can reduce security risk but impose substantial collateral disruption, whereas conservative intervention may preserve short-term availability while allowing attacker footholds to deepen. Autonomous cyber defense is therefore fundamentally a problem of managing competing operational objectives under deployment constraints, not merely maximizing a single reward signal.

This deployment perspective matters because defensive success in practice is inherently preference-dependent. Different organizations, and even the same organization at different moments, may rationally prefer different trade-offs among security effectiveness, service continuity, and defense overhead. A hospital during active service degradation, a cloud provider during peak traffic hours, and an enterprise responding overnight need not choose the same operating point. As a result, a single fixed defense policy is unlikely to be suitable across all deployment settings. What is needed instead is a policy set that spans meaningful trade-offs and supports policy assignment under changing operational preferences and strict operational limits.

Recent progress in reinforcement learning has made autonomous cyber defense increasingly tractable in simulated environments. However, most existing learning-based approaches still optimize a scalarized objective or aim to learn a single deployable policy. Such formulations are a poor match for cyber defense deployment. They collapse heterogeneous operational requirements into one reward or one operating point, making it difficult to adapt to organizations with different tolerances for disruption, residual risk, and intervention cost. More importantly, in cyber operations, the practical value of a defense method is not only whether it can produce a strong policy on average, but whether it can provide multiple feasible policies that remain usable under strict deployment constraints.

These limitations motivate a constrained multi-objective view of autonomous cyber defense. Rather than reducing all defensive outcomes to a single scalar objective, we formulate blue-team defense as a constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning problem with three deployment-relevant objectives: security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense overhead. We additionally impose operational constraints to capture unacceptable behavior, such as excessive disruption or overly aggressive intervention. Under this formulation, the goal is not to learn one universally best policy, but to construct a deployable policy set that approximates the Pareto trade-off frontier and supports flexible policy selection for different deployment preferences.

In this paper, we study this problem in CybORG~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp} and adopt a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework for autonomous blue-team defense. In the first stage, we initialize a set of preference-diverse defense policies. In the second stage, we expand the policy set through constraint-aware Pareto optimization in order to improve frontier quality and deployment flexibility. This design is motivated by a practical observation: in cyber defense, discovering a strong candidate set is not the same as achieving strong final deployment. A method may appear effective at the set level while still failing to provide feasible or desirable policies once strict operational constraints and concrete deployment preferences are imposed.

Accordingly, we evaluate autonomous cyber defense from two complementary perspectives. At the candidate-set level, we measure Pareto-front quality, expected utility, and preference coverage. At the deployment level, we assess feasible policy assignment under strict constraints and report cyber-semantic outcomes such as critical-asset protection, attack-impact suppression, service disruption, and defense overhead. This separation between set quality and deployment quality reflects the central practical question of autonomous defense: not only whether a method can discover good trade-offs, but whether it can offer deployable choices for different operational regimes.

Our results show that a constrained Pareto policy-set formulation provides a more realistic basis for autonomous cyber defense than single-policy or purely scalarized baselines. By explicitly modeling deployment trade-offs and evaluating both frontier quality and constrained deployment behavior, we show that autonomous cyber defense is better framed as constrained trade-off management over deployable policy sets than as single-policy reward maximization.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate autonomous cyber defense as a constrained multi-objective sequential decision problem with three operational objectives---security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense overhead---under deployment-relevant operational constraints.
    \item We instantiate a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework in CybORG to construct deployment-aware defense policy sets rather than a single fixed operating point.
    \item We introduce a deployment-aware evaluation protocol that explicitly separates candidate-set quality from final deployment quality, combining Pareto metrics with strict-constraint policy assignment and cyber-semantic outcomes.
    \item We provide empirical evidence that constrained Pareto policy sets yield stronger deployment flexibility and more useful feasible choices than scalarization-based and preference-conditioned baselines for autonomous cyber defense.
\end{itemize}


\section{Threat Model and Deployment Assumptions}
\label{sec:threat-deployment}

We study a simulated enterprise-defense setting in which a blue defender responds to an adversarial red process over a finite horizon. The purpose of this section is to make explicit the operational assumptions under which the learned policies are trained, evaluated, and selected for deployment. Our focus is not only whether a defender can accumulate high reward, but whether it can prevent attacker progress toward critical assets while preserving service continuity and staying within operationally acceptable response limits.

\subsection{Attacker Model}

The attacker is represented by the red process implemented in the CybORG environment~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp}. Within a given training or evaluation regime, the red process is standardized and fixed. It may generate stochastic trajectories through the environment, but it is not a co-learning adversary that adapts its strategy online in response to the learned blue policy. This design choice allows us to isolate the quality of blue-team decision making rather than confounding it with equilibrium learning or online red--blue adaptation.

The attacker's objective is to progress through the enterprise network toward higher-value states and critical assets over an episode horizon of \(T=100\) steps. Operationally, this captures lateral movement, persistence, and attack-impact behavior that can result in compromised critical hosts or service-impacting events. The defender's task is therefore not merely to react to isolated alerts, but to make long-horizon response decisions that slow or suppress attacker progress before critical impact accumulates.

To probe limited robustness beyond the default red process, we additionally evaluate a held-out attacker shift without retraining. This stress test is not intended to establish full attacker-robust generalization. Instead, it measures whether policies trained under the default red process retain useful behavior when directly evaluated against a different red-side behavior.

\subsection{Defender Observability and Response Space}

The blue defender operates under partial observability. At each time step, it receives a cyber-specific observation summarizing defender-visible information such as host status, alerts, and telemetry exposed by the environment. The defender does not observe the full latent cyber state, the attacker's internal plan, or future attacker actions. It must therefore infer risk from incomplete evidence and choose actions whose consequences unfold over multiple time steps.

The defender selects one response action from the available CybORG action set at each step. These actions include analysis, removal, restoration, and related host-level interventions. They differ not only in security effect but also in operational side effects. Some actions are relatively local and low cost, while others can be disruptive, consume response capacity, or trigger recovery overhead. We therefore treat defensive actions as deployment-sensitive interventions rather than abstract control moves.

This distinction is important for autonomous cyber defense. A policy that suppresses attacker progress by repeatedly restoring or aggressively intervening on hosts may look strong from a containment perspective, but it may be unacceptable in a service-sensitive deployment. Conversely, a policy that avoids intervention may preserve continuity in the short term while allowing attacker footholds to deepen. The defender's action space therefore naturally induces trade-offs among security effectiveness, service continuity, and defense overhead.

\subsection{Deployment Preferences, Limits, and Regimes}

We distinguish between \emph{preferences} and \emph{hard deployment limits}. Preferences specify how an organization ranks competing objectives such as containment, continuity, and cost. For example, an organization under active incident containment may prefer stronger security actions, whereas a service-sensitive organization during peak load may prioritize continuity. These preferences are later represented by a deployment-time weight vector over the three objectives.

Hard limits are different. They define behaviors that remain unacceptable regardless of preference. Examples include exceeding a disruption budget, relying too heavily on high-impact actions, or sustaining intervention patterns that a human operator would reject. These limits are later formalized as feasibility constraints over business disruption and costly intervention. A policy may have high scalar utility under a particular preference but still be excluded from deployment if it violates these operational limits.

This separation is central to our setting. Standard scalarization can express what a defender values, but it does not by itself enforce what a defender is unwilling to tolerate. We therefore model deployment as constrained policy selection: a policy must first satisfy operational limits and only then be compared according to the organization's preference over security, business continuity, and cost.

In the experiments, we instantiate deployment regimes through common constraint thresholds. A loose regime represents moderate tolerance for disruption and high-impact intervention, while a tight regime represents stricter deployment conditions with a narrower acceptable response space. These regimes are protocol-level abstractions of realistic operating contexts such as peak service hours, after-hours response, or active incident containment.

\subsection{Deployment-Time Policy Use}

The learned object in this paper is a policy set rather than a single universal defense policy. Training produces an archive of candidate blue-team policies with different operational trade-offs. At deployment time, an organization specifies its preference over the three objectives together with the applicable business and cost limits. The system then filters the archive to feasible policies and assigns the feasible policy with the highest utility for that preference.

This deployment protocol assumes no retraining during policy assignment. The defender does not learn a new policy for each organization, each preference, or each evaluation episode. Instead, the policy set is constructed offline, and deployment-time adaptation occurs through feasible policy selection. This assumption reflects the practical goal of providing a reusable set of operating points that can support different regimes without requiring new training whenever operational priorities change.

\subsection{Scope and Non-Goals}

Our setting is a controlled cyber-range model for studying deployment-aware blue-team learning. It is not a fully general stochastic game, and we do not attempt to compute adaptive red--blue equilibria. The attacker process is standardized within each regime, and the held-out attacker evaluation is treated as a limited stress test rather than a comprehensive robustness guarantee.

We also do not claim that the learned policies are ready for unsupervised deployment in live enterprise networks. The operational constraints used in this paper are proxy abstractions of disruption tolerance and response overhead; they do not yet encode organization-specific service-level agreements, approval workflows, legal requirements, or asset-criticality policies. Our claims are therefore intentionally bounded: we evaluate whether deployment-aware policy-set learning provides more useful constrained blue-team choices than scalarized or single-policy alternatives under a shared CybORG protocol.

\section{Constrained Multi-Objective Formulation of Blue-Team Defense}
\label{sec:formulation}

We now formalize the deployment setting introduced above as a constrained multi-objective blue-team decision problem. The formulation is designed to preserve the operational semantics of autonomous cyber defense: a useful policy should suppress attacker progress, preserve service continuity, and avoid excessive response overhead. The key distinction is between \emph{preferences}, which determine how an organization values different trade-offs, and \emph{hard deployment constraints}, which define behavior that is unacceptable regardless of preference.

\subsection{Partially Observable Blue-Team Decision Process}

We model the cyber-defense environment as a finite-horizon partially observable decision process,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}
=
\langle
\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{A}, P, Z, \mathbf{r}, \mathbf{c}, \gamma
\rangle ,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the latent cyber state space, \(\mathcal{O}\) is the defender observation space, \(\mathcal{A}\) is the defender action space, \(P\) is the transition kernel, \(Z\) is the observation kernel, \(\mathbf{r}\) is a vector-valued reward signal, \(\mathbf{c}\) is a vector of constraint signals, and \(\gamma \in [0,1]\) is a discount factor used during learning.

At time \(t\), the defender receives a partial observation
\begin{equation}
o_t \sim Z(\cdot \mid s_t), \qquad o_t \in \mathcal{O},
\end{equation}
selects a blue-team response action
\begin{equation}
a_t \in \mathcal{A},
\end{equation}
and the environment evolves according to
\begin{equation}
s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t).
\end{equation}
The transition kernel captures both the standardized red process and the downstream effects of blue-team intervention. Thus, attacker progress, service impact, and recovery consequences are path dependent. A policy must therefore be evaluated through rollout-level outcomes rather than through isolated single-step reward alone.

A trajectory is denoted by
\begin{equation}
\tau = (s_0,o_0,a_0,\ldots,s_T,o_T),
\end{equation}
where \(T=100\) in our CybORG experiments. A blue-team policy \(\pi(a \mid o)\) maps defender observations to response actions under partial observability.

\subsection{Operational Objective Signals}

We use three deployment-relevant objectives: \emph{security effectiveness}, \emph{service continuity}, and \emph{defense overhead}. Rather than defining these objectives only as abstract reward dimensions, we instantiate them through cyber-semantic rollout summaries that can be logged and interpreted by a defender:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(H_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\): final number of critical compromised hosts;
    \item \(N_{\mathrm{impact}}(\tau)\): realized critical-impact events;
    \item \(D_{\mathrm{svc}}(\tau)\): cumulative service-disruption proxy;
    \item \(N_{\mathrm{restore}}(\tau)\): number of restore or recovery actions;
    \item \(N_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau)\): total intervention count, including analysis, removal, restoration, and related actions;
    \item \(N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\tau)\): number of expensive or high-disruption actions.
\end{itemize}

Using positive coefficients \(\alpha_{\cdot}, \beta_{\cdot}, \eta_{\cdot} > 0\), we define the trajectory-level objective vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{R}(\tau)
=
\left[
R^{\mathrm{sec}}(\tau),
R^{\mathrm{svc}}(\tau),
R^{\mathrm{ovh}}(\tau)
\right],
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
R^{\mathrm{sec}}(\tau)
&=
-\alpha_h H_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
-\alpha_i N_{\mathrm{impact}}(\tau),
\\
R^{\mathrm{svc}}(\tau)
&=
-\beta_d D_{\mathrm{svc}}(\tau)
-\beta_r N_{\mathrm{restore}}(\tau),
\\
R^{\mathrm{ovh}}(\tau)
&=
-\eta_n N_{\mathrm{int}}(\tau)
-\eta_e N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\tau).
\end{align}

The sign convention makes larger values preferable for all objectives. A policy is better on the security objective when it leaves fewer critical hosts compromised and triggers fewer attack-impact events. It is better on the service-continuity objective when it causes less disruption and requires fewer recovery actions. It is better on the defense-overhead objective when it uses fewer interventions and relies less on high-impact responses.

For a policy \(\pi\), the expected objective vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}
\left[
\mathbf{R}(\tau)
\right]
=
\left[
J^{\mathrm{sec}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{svc}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{ovh}}(\pi)
\right].
\end{equation}
The same objectives can be implemented through per-step vector rewards during learning, but the deployment interpretation is defined at the rollout level.

The use of both \(N_{\mathrm{restore}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{int}}\) is intentional. Restore actions reflect recovery churn and service instability, so they contribute to the service-continuity objective. They also require operator effort and response capacity, so they may also contribute to the intervention count. This overlap captures two distinct operational meanings of recovery activity rather than accidental double counting.

\subsection{Hard Deployment Constraints}

Some response patterns should not be treated only as soft reward penalties. In deployment, a policy may be unacceptable even if it achieves high utility under a particular preference. We therefore introduce hard feasibility constraints over rollout-level signals.

In the general case, let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}(\tau)
=
\left[
C_1(\tau),\ldots,C_M(\tau)
\right]
\end{equation}
denote deployment-relevant constraint summaries. A policy is feasible with respect to budget vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) if
\begin{equation}
C_m(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}
\left[
C_m(\tau)
\right]
\leq
\delta_m,
\qquad
m=1,\ldots,M.
\end{equation}

In our CybORG instantiation, we use two deployment-facing constraints:
\begin{align}
C_{\mathrm{biz}}(\pi)
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}
\left[
D_{\mathrm{svc}}(\tau)
\right]
\leq
\delta_{\mathrm{biz}},
\\
C_{\mathrm{cost}}(\pi)
&=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}
\left[
N_{\mathrm{exp}}(\tau)
\right]
\leq
\delta_{\mathrm{cost}}.
\end{align}
The first constraint represents business or service-disruption tolerance. The second represents tolerance for high-impact defensive actions. These two signals are used as hard constraints because they correspond directly to deployment rejection conditions: a real operator may reject a policy that causes too much service disruption or repeatedly uses disruptive remediation, even if that policy improves containment.

Other signals, such as intervention count or restore count, still affect the objective vector and therefore influence preference-based utility. They are not all used as hard feasibility gates because our deployment protocol separates broad operational quality from strict rejection thresholds.

\subsection{Feasible Preference-Based Policy Assignment}

Deployment preferences are represented by a weight vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w} \in \Delta_3
=
\left\{
\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
:
\sum_{i=1}^{3} w_i = 1
\right\},
\end{equation}
where the three dimensions correspond to security effectiveness, service continuity, and defense overhead.

Because the three objective dimensions may have different numerical scales, we define deployment utility over a calibrated objective vector \(\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\). This calibration is fixed across methods within the evaluation protocol; when raw returns are used directly, \(\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)=\mathbf{J}(\pi)\). The utility of policy \(\pi\) under preference \(\mathbf{w}\) is
\begin{equation}
U(\pi;\mathbf{w})
=
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
\end{equation}

Let \(\Pi\) denote a learned candidate policy set. Under deployment budgets \((\delta_{\mathrm{biz}}, \delta_{\mathrm{cost}})\), the feasible subset is
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}
=
\left\{
\pi \in \Pi
:
C_{\mathrm{biz}}(\pi) \leq \delta_{\mathrm{biz}},
\;
C_{\mathrm{cost}}(\pi) \leq \delta_{\mathrm{cost}}
\right\}.
\end{equation}
If \(\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}\neq \emptyset\), the deployed policy is selected by
\begin{equation}
\pi^{\star}(\mathbf{w})
=
\arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}}
U(\pi;\mathbf{w}).
\end{equation}

If \(\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}=\emptyset\), the assignment is marked infeasible for that preference and deployment regime. For diagnostic reporting, one may additionally record the least-violating candidate, but such a candidate is not counted as a feasible deployment. This convention is important for evaluating tight deployment regimes, where a method may produce a high-quality Pareto archive but still fail to provide an admissible policy under strict thresholds.

\subsection{Policy-Set Learning Goal}

The learning problem is therefore not to find a single universally optimal blue-team policy. Instead, the goal is to learn a finite policy set \(\Pi\) that satisfies three deployment-oriented requirements: it should cover meaningful trade-offs among the three objectives, contain policies that satisfy hard operational constraints, and support feasible preference-based assignment without retraining.

This formulation directly motivates the learning framework in the next section. Stage 1 constructs a diverse initial archive of blue-team behaviors, while Stage 2 expands that archive toward additional feasible Pareto trade-offs under the business and cost constraints defined above.

\section{Learning Deployable Defense Policy Sets}
\label{sec:learning}

In this section, we introduce the learning framework for constructing deployable defense policy sets. The objective is to learn a set of policies that are not only effective in managing trade-offs between competing objectives but are also deployable under strict operational constraints. The learning process consists of two main stages: \emph{Pareto Initialization} and \emph{Pareto Extension}, which together ensure that the learned policy set covers a broad range of feasible operational regimes.

\subsection{Pareto Initialization: Learning Preference-Diverse Policies}

The first stage of the learning process, Pareto Initialization, is designed to provide a diverse set of initial policies that span the preference space. In this stage, multiple policies are trained in parallel, each optimized for a specific preference vector \( \mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{init}} \), where \( \mathcal{W}_{\text{init}} \) is a set of predefined preferences sampled from the preference space \( \Delta_3 \).

For each preference vector \( \mathbf{w} \), we solve the following multi-objective optimization problem:

\[
J_{\mathbf{w}}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{r}_t \right],
\]
where \( \mathbf{r}_t = [r_t^{\text{sec}}, r_t^{\text{svc}}, r_t^{\text{ovh}}] \) is the per-step reward vector, and \( \gamma \) is the discount factor. This objective maximizes the utility under each preference, producing an initial archive of policies \( \Pi_{\text{init}} = \{\pi_{\mathbf{w}} \mid \mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{init}} \} \).

The key idea of this stage is to capture a diverse set of policies that represent different trade-offs between security, service continuity, and defense overhead. The resulting policies are preference-irrelevant, meaning they are not tied to a specific set of preferences and can be adapted to new preferences during the subsequent stages of the learning process.

\subsection{Pareto Extension: Expanding the Policy Set Under Constraints}

The second stage, Pareto Extension, focuses on refining the initial policy set \( \Pi_{\text{init}} \) by expanding it to cover more diverse operating points along the Pareto front. In this stage, we employ constrained optimization to iteratively improve the policies and explore additional regions of the Pareto front while satisfying operational constraints.

We optimize one objective at a time while ensuring that the other objectives remain above predefined thresholds. The constrained optimization problem at iteration \(r\) for objective \(l\) is formulated as:

\[
\pi_{r+1} = \arg \max_{\pi} \, J_l(\pi) \quad \text{subject to} \quad J_i(\pi) \geq \kappa_i, \, i \neq l, \quad C_{\text{biz}}(\pi) \leq \delta_{\text{biz}}, \quad C_{\text{cost}}(\pi) \leq \delta_{\text{cost}},
\]
where \( J_l(\pi) \) is the reward for objective \(l\), \( \kappa_i \) are the thresholds for the other objectives, and \( C_{\text{biz}}(\pi) \) and \( C_{\text{cost}}(\pi) \) are the business disruption and cost constraints, respectively. This formulation allows the policies to be refined toward optimal trade-offs in different objective directions.

The Pareto front is extended by selecting policies based on their crowding distance, which indicates how densely packed policies are along the Pareto front. Policies with larger crowding distances are prioritized because they fill more sparsely populated regions of the Pareto front, improving the diversity of the policy set. The policy selection is repeated until a sufficiently diverse set of policies is obtained.

\subsection{Policy Selection and Assignment}

Policy selection plays a crucial role in ensuring that the final policy set \( \Pi \) contains only the most relevant policies for deployment. During both Pareto Initialization and Pareto Extension, we use the crowd distance criterion to select policies that are far from each other on the Pareto front, thus improving the diversity of the learned policies.

The policy selection procedure is summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Select policies with the largest crowd distance to ensure they occupy distinct regions on the Pareto front.
    \item Perform constrained optimization to expand the set by refining policies in underexplored regions.
    \item Repeat the selection and expansion process until the policy set contains a sufficient number of high-quality, diverse policies.
\end{itemize}

At deployment time, given a preference vector \( \mathbf{w} \), the defender selects the policy that maximizes the utility function \( U(\pi; \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi) \), where \( \tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi) \) is the normalized objective vector. The policy selection process is constrained to only consider feasible policies that meet the deployment thresholds \( C_{\text{biz}}(\pi) \leq \delta_{\text{biz}} \) and \( C_{\text{cost}}(\pi) \leq \delta_{\text{cost}} \).

If no feasible policy is available, the assignment is marked as infeasible for the given preference and deployment regime. In such cases, the least-violating policy can be reported for diagnostic purposes, but it will not be considered a valid deployment.

\subsection{Efficiency and Time Complexity}

The proposed method is designed to be computationally efficient while maintaining high-quality performance. In particular, we note that the C-MORL approach scales well with the number of objectives, as the time complexity is linear in the number of objectives. The training process involves two main steps: the initial policy training and the constrained optimization for Pareto extension. The expected running time for the entire process is \( O(nKN) \), where \(n\) is the number of objectives, \(K\) is the number of Pareto extension steps, and \(N\) is the number of policies selected for extension.

This time complexity is significantly more efficient than methods that rely on solving complex optimization problems for each grid point in the objective space, such as the epsilon-constraint method, which has exponential complexity. C-MORL’s efficient policy selection and extension mechanism enable it to handle complex MORL tasks with multiple objectives, making it a promising approach for real-world autonomous defense systems.

\section{Experimental Testbed and Evaluation Questions}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{CybORG Instantiation}
\label{subsec:setup-environment}

We instantiate the formulation in the CybORG cyber-defense environment~\cite{baillie2020cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}. CybORG provides a partially observable attack--defense process with cyber-specific observations, defender actions, attacker progression, and critical assets. We use the implemented V2.4 protocol in Scenario2 with the default \texttt{bline} red process and an episode horizon of $T=100$ steps.

This setup is intentionally controlled. It is realistic enough to expose lateral movement, enterprise footholds, and critical-host compromise, yet stable enough to support fair cross-method comparison under shared preferences and shared deployment thresholds. Our goal is not equilibrium analysis against a co-learning attacker. Instead, we study whether a defender can learn a \emph{deployable set} of cyber-defense operating points under a standardized attack--defense process.

\subsection{Four-Objective Instantiation}
\label{subsec:setup-objectives}

Our experiments use four defender-side objectives: \emph{security effectiveness}, \emph{business continuity}, \emph{defense cost}, and \emph{critical-asset safety}. Security rewards suppression of attacker progress and reduction of realized impact. Business rewards preservation of service continuity and lower disruption. Cost rewards lower intervention burden and less reliance on expensive actions. The fourth objective, critical-asset safety, explicitly targets tail-risk behavior around enterprise footholds and critical hosts.

This fourth objective is part of the optimization problem itself; it is not an auxiliary post-hoc penalty. The deployment-time semantic audit described below is used to validate whether learned policies exhibit the intended behavior, but it is not a fifth training objective. Operational constraints remain deployment-time limits on business disruption and costly intervention, and the Stage~2 expansion procedure is explicitly aware of these limits during policy-set construction.

\subsection{Compared Methods}
\label{subsec:setup-baselines}

The main-text comparison is intentionally minimal and strictly same-protocol. We report four methods that were all executed under the current four-objective protocol: \emph{Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set} (our method), \emph{Stage-1 Policy Archive}, \emph{Weighted-Sum Scalarization}, and \emph{Unconstrained Stage-2 Policy Set}. Table~\ref{tab:set-quality} compares the first three methods at the candidate-set level, while Table~\ref{tab:deployment-quality} adds the unconstrained Stage~2 ablation for deployment-time comparison.

\emph{Weighted-Sum Scalarization} is the scalarization baseline. \emph{Stage-1 Policy Archive} measures how far a preference-diverse initialization archive can go without Stage~2 expansion. \emph{Unconstrained Stage-2 Policy Set} keeps Stage~2 expansion but removes explicit constraint-aware shaping, letting us isolate the role of constrained expansion. Older baselines such as PCN, preference-conditioned baselines, Lagrangian PPO, and single-objective methods are not included in the current main-text comparison because they were not rerun under the same four-objective protocol; mixing them with the current results would not be a fair comparison.

\subsection{Evaluation Questions and Metrics}
\label{subsec:setup-metrics}

Our evaluation has three layers: candidate-set quality in four-dimensional objective space, constrained deployment performance under shared thresholds, and cyber-semantic safety under modeled attack--defense dynamics.

\paragraph{RQ1: Does the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set learn a stronger four-objective candidate set?}
At the set level, we report hypervolume (HV), expected utility (EU), coverage ratio, number of unique assigned policies, and number of retained Pareto records~\cite{hayes2022guide,liu2025efficient}.

\paragraph{RQ2: Does the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set achieve stronger constrained deployment performance?}
At deployment time, we report security return, business return, cost return, feasible rate, mean constraint violation, final critical compromised hosts, and critical-impact count under a shared-threshold assignment protocol.

\paragraph{RQ3: Does the method improve security semantics rather than only scalar metrics?}
We run replay-level semantic audits that partition trajectories into \emph{enterprise foothold}, \emph{pre-critical}, and \emph{critical breach} phases. The audit reports risk tiers (Tier~0 safe, Tier~1 near-miss, Tier~2 transient critical breach, Tier~3 persistent critical breach), questionable action rules Q2--Q5, and pre-critical action-family statistics.

\paragraph{RQ4: What do Stage~2 expansion and constraint-aware extension contribute?}
We compare the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set against the Stage-1 Policy Archive to isolate the value of Stage~2 expansion, and against the Unconstrained Stage-2 Policy Set to isolate the value of explicit constraint-aware shaping during expansion.

All methods are evaluated on the same shared preference grid over the four-objective simplex. For each preference vector, a final deployed policy is selected using the same deployment-time assignment rule and the same shared business/cost thresholds.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:setup-impl}

All main-text results use three seeds $\{7,11,19\}$. The same seeds are used for candidate-set evaluation, constrained deployment evaluation, and semantic replay audit. For policy-set methods, Stage~2 starts from the Stage~1 archive, expands selected parent policies, and evaluates final deployment through the same shared-threshold assignment protocol. Detailed hyperparameters, semantic rule definitions, and configuration files are deferred to the artifact package.

Table~\ref{tab:deployment-quality} answers RQ2. The relevant question is not whether one method can obtain the numerically strongest raw return in isolation, but whether it can produce a stronger \emph{deployable compromise} under shared business and cost thresholds. On that reading, the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set is the strongest method in the comparison set. The contrast against Weighted-Sum Scalarization is decisive. \subsection{Cyber-Semantic Safety Analysis}
\label{subsec:results-semantics}

The strongest result of V2.4 is not only in reward space. We also evaluate the selected policy for each seed with a replay-level semantic audit that models the attack--defense process explicitly. The audit tracks whether the attacker has established an enterprise foothold, whether the interaction has entered a pre-critical window, and whether a true critical breach ever occurs. It then summarizes each rollout with risk tiers, questionable action rules, and phase-specific action-family statistics.

At the aggregate level, the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set eliminates critical breach in the three-seed evaluation: the selected policies achieve $0.0$ ever-critical-breach rate, $0.0$ persistent-critical-breach rate, $0.0$ mean critical dwell steps, and $0.0$ high-confidence bad-run rate. The same audit also shows that questionable critical-time behavior is almost entirely removed: Q2, Q3, and Q5 all drop to $0.0$, while Q4 is reduced to $0.0021$. Relative to the baseline, these changes are not incremental; they correspond to removing catastrophic failure modes that were previously common.

The process-level evidence is equally important. Across all three seeds, the semantic audit shows that pre-critical defense becomes fully restore-focused: the aggregate pre-critical action-family rate is $1.0$ for \texttt{restore} and $0.0$ for \texttt{decoy}, while the pre-critical compromised-target focus rate is also $1.0$. The seed-level casebooks show the same qualitative pattern: the attacker reaches an enterprise foothold, and the defender responds by restoring the compromised enterprise host before the interaction ever enters a critical-present state. This is why the method should be described as \emph{breach-free but not risk-free}. Tier~2 and Tier~3 are eliminated, but Tier~1 Near-Miss remains $1.0$ and Tier~0 Safe remains $0.0$. Our strongest supported claim is therefore that V2.4 eliminates critical breach while preserving near-miss risk, not that it removes all cyber risk.

\subsection{Ablation: Why Stage~2 and Why Constraint-Aware Extension?}
\label{subsec:results-ablation}

The ablation question has two parts. First, does Stage~2 expansion help beyond a preference-diverse initialization archive? Second, does explicit constraint-aware shaping matter during expansion?

Comparing the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set against the Stage-1 Policy Archive shows that Stage~2 is materially useful at both the set and deployment levels. The full method improves hypervolume and expected utility, while also improving feasible rate and sharply reducing mean violation under the shared-threshold deployment protocol. Comparing the constrained and unconstrained Stage~2 variants shows a different effect: removing explicit constraint awareness can yield a more aggressive operating point in raw return space, but it substantially worsens deployment acceptability and high-disruption behavior. In short, Stage~2 expansion improves the archive, and constraint-aware expansion determines whether the deployed points remain operationally acceptable.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Defense and Cyber Ranges}
\label{subsec:related-cyber}

Autonomous cyber defense studies sequential blue-team decision making in environments where attacker progress, partial observability, and action side effects all matter. Cyber ranges such as CybORG have made this line of work reproducible by providing standardized defender observations, action spaces, attacker processes, and scenario-driven evaluation settings~\cite{baillie2020cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}. Our work builds on this line, but shifts the emphasis from learning a single strong defense policy to learning a set of deployable blue-team operating points.

\subsection{Security Work on Defensive Trade-offs}
\label{subsec:related-security}

A large body of security research foregrounds operational trade-offs rather than abstract optimization metrics. Classic security-systems papers such as Bohatei frame the problem around practical deployment constraints like scalability, responsiveness, and adversary resilience, with the system and evaluation story carrying the paper before a consolidated late related-work section~\cite{fayaz2015bohatei}. Defensive deception work studies how decoys and psychological signals alter attacker behavior under controlled penetration settings~\cite{fergusonwalter2021deception}. Recent system and planning papers treat attack and defense through concrete security artifacts such as exploit chains, attack paths, and action semantics~\cite{depasquale2024chainreactor}. Other work uses game-theoretic or economic models to reason about how different security incentives or interventions affect aggregate loss~\cite{hastings2025investment}. Work on proactive defenses against LLM-based agents likewise frames the defender's objective in terms of concrete operational outcomes such as delay, detection, and disruption of malicious behavior~\cite{ayzenshteyn2025cloak}. Our paper is aligned with this security-first tradition: the learning machinery is important, but it is subordinate to the question of whether a defender obtains better deployable choices.

\subsection{MORL and Constrained Optimization}
\label{subsec:related-morl}

Multi-objective reinforcement learning studies sequential decision problems with multiple, potentially conflicting objectives~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022guide}. A central distinction is between preference-conditioned policies and explicit policy-set approaches. Conditioned methods encode the preference vector directly into the model~\cite{yang2019generalized,reymond2022pcn,lu2023morl}, while policy-set methods explicitly construct a Pareto archive and assign policies at evaluation time~\cite{liu2025efficient}. Constrained reinforcement learning addresses the orthogonal question of whether high-return policies remain acceptable under safety, cost, or operational budgets~\cite{achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo,paternain2019zeroduality}. Our work combines these views in a cyber-defense setting: we use constrained multi-objective optimization not to train one policy for one preference, but to discover additional feasible blue-team operating points that remain meaningful at deployment time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Results Support}
\label{subsec:discussion-claims}

The empirical evidence supports a specific claim: deployment-aware policy-set learning provides a stronger basis for autonomous cyber defense than scalarized or reduced alternatives when the goal is to support heterogeneous deployment preferences under operational constraints. The evidence now appears at three aligned levels: improved four-objective candidate-set quality, stronger constrained deployment performance, and markedly better cyber-semantic safety under replay-level audit.

The paper does \emph{not} support a stronger claim of universal dominance or complete risk elimination. The unconstrained Stage~2 variant can still look better on some raw return dimensions, and the selected V2.4 policies remain in Tier~1 Near-Miss rather than Tier~0 Safe. These boundaries matter because they show that the present contribution is a breach-free, constraint-aware deployment perspective for blue-team defense, not a complete solution to all cyber-defense decision settings.

\subsection{Limitations and Practical Implications}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

This work has three main limitations. First, the attacker is represented by a standardized process rather than a fully adaptive learning opponent. This is appropriate for isolating blue-team decision quality, but it leaves open the question of how policy-set learning behaves under broader attacker variation. Second, the operational constraints remain proxy-based abstractions of deployment limits. Although they capture disruption tolerance and intervention burden, they do not yet encode organization-specific SLAs, approval workflows, or asset-criticality rules. Third, the current evaluation is centered on CybORG and a fixed family of scenarios.

At the same time, the operational implication of the current results is clear. In blue-team settings, the useful object is often not a single best policy, but a set of feasible responses spanning multiple operating regimes. A defender under high-pressure containment and a defender in a service-sensitive environment may need different acceptable policies. Policy-set learning addresses exactly this need.

A second practical implication concerns interpretability. By tying the four objectives to observable rollout summaries such as final critical compromises, impact events, intervention counts, expensive-action frequency, and critical-safety semantics, the framework makes deployment reasoning more legible than a monolithic scalar reward. Even when the underlying optimization is complex, the resulting operating points can still be discussed in the language of concrete defender outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied autonomous cyber defense as a constrained four-objective blue-team decision problem in which defenders must balance security effectiveness, business continuity, defense cost, and critical-asset safety under explicit deployment limits. We instantiated this view in CybORG with a two-stage learning framework that constructs a deployment-aware set of feasible blue-team policies rather than one fixed response policy.

The resulting evidence is both conceptual and empirical. Deployment-aware policy-set learning improves candidate-set quality, yields stronger constrained deployment performance than scalarized and reduced baselines under a shared protocol, and eliminates critical breach in replay-level semantic audit while preserving near-miss risk. More broadly, the paper argues that autonomous cyber defense should be treated not only as policy optimization, but as constrained deployment under heterogeneous operational preferences and explicit critical-safety semantics.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

This paper studies defensive automation in a simulated cyber range. All experiments are conducted in CybORG; we do not deploy the learned policies on live enterprise systems, scan third-party infrastructure, or conduct offensive activity against real users or organizations. The primary ethical risk lies in over-claiming the readiness of autonomous defense. Our evaluation therefore emphasizes controlled attacker processes, explicit operational constraints, and the limitations of held-out attacker shift. We do not claim that the current system can be safely deployed without human oversight in production networks.
