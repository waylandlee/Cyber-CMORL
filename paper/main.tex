\begin{abstract}
LLM-enabled cyber attacks pose a realistic threat in cyberspace, and the autonomous cyber defense (ACD) is a promising technology for confronting AI-enabled attacks.
However, deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based decision-making for ACD in preventing lateral-movement and responding ransomware in IT\&OT scenarios is a time-sensitive sequential decision problem.
Existing methods typically collapse the problem into a scalarized reward or a single operating point, which ignored the disruptions of critical services, exhaustion of defending resource, and rising critical risks in hindering sophisticated attackers.

This paper presents a constrained multi-objective DRL method for ACD agents by formulating the defender with four deployable objectives: \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}.
Instead of maximizing a single reward policy, our method constructs a deployable policy set through cyber-semantic objectives and constraint-aware Pareto expansion, which different operational preferences can be realized without re-training.
We propose a two-stage Pareto-oriented framework that initializes preference-diverse policies and expands them via constraint-aware Pareto optimization to yield a deployable policy set for ACD.
By implemented in the CybORG++ simulators, our method achieves \textbf{$3.9\times$} higher hypervolume than weighted-sum scalarization, raises expected utility from $-161.57$ to $-41.92$, increases feasible deployment rate to $0.64$, and reduces mean constraint violation from $93.54$ to $0.09$, without critical compromised hosts and critical-impact events.
These experiments show that a constrained trade-off management over multi-objective policy sets can enable the ACD agent with more deployable guarantees than straightforward single-policy reward maximization.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

AI-enabled attack--defense interaction is making autonomous cyber defense a time-sensitive sequential decision problem rather than a single-objective control task. In realistic enterprise networks, defenders must respond to attacker progression under operational pressure: actions that suppress compromise may also interrupt critical services, degrade business workflows, or consume limited response capacity. During lateral movement or ransomware-style propagation, this tension becomes especially severe. Aggressive containment can reduce security risk but impose substantial collateral disruption, whereas conservative intervention may preserve short-term availability while allowing attacker footholds to deepen. Autonomous cyber defense is therefore fundamentally a problem of managing competing operational objectives under deployment constraints, not merely maximizing a single reward signal.

This deployment perspective matters because defensive success in practice is inherently preference-dependent. Different organizations, and even the same organization at different moments, may rationally prefer different trade-offs among security effectiveness, service continuity, and defense overhead. A hospital during active service degradation, a cloud provider during peak traffic hours, and an enterprise responding overnight need not choose the same operating point. As a result, a single fixed defense policy is unlikely to be suitable across all deployment settings. What is needed instead is a policy set that spans meaningful trade-offs and supports policy assignment under changing operational preferences and strict operational limits.

Recent progress in reinforcement learning has made autonomous cyber defense increasingly tractable in simulated environments. However, most existing learning-based approaches still optimize a scalarized objective or aim to learn a single deployable policy. Such formulations are a poor match for cyber defense deployment. They collapse heterogeneous operational requirements into one reward or one operating point, making it difficult to adapt to organizations with different tolerances for disruption, residual risk, and intervention cost. More importantly, in cyber operations, the practical value of a defense method is not only whether it can produce a strong policy on average, but whether it can provide multiple feasible policies that remain usable under strict deployment constraints.

These limitations motivate a constrained multi-objective view of autonomous cyber defense. Rather than reducing all defensive outcomes to a single scalar objective, we formulate blue-team defense as a constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning problem with three deployment-relevant objectives: security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense overhead. We additionally impose operational constraints to capture unacceptable behavior, such as excessive disruption or overly aggressive intervention. Under this formulation, the goal is not to learn one universally best policy, but to construct a deployable policy set that approximates the Pareto trade-off frontier and supports flexible policy selection for different deployment preferences.

In this paper, we study this problem in CybORG~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp} and adopt a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework for autonomous blue-team defense. In the first stage, we initialize a set of preference-diverse defense policies. In the second stage, we expand the policy set through constraint-aware Pareto optimization in order to improve frontier quality and deployment flexibility. This design is motivated by a practical observation: in cyber defense, discovering a strong candidate set is not the same as achieving strong final deployment. A method may appear effective at the set level while still failing to provide feasible or desirable policies once strict operational constraints and concrete deployment preferences are imposed.

Accordingly, we evaluate autonomous cyber defense from two complementary perspectives. At the candidate-set level, we measure Pareto-front quality, expected utility, and preference coverage. At the deployment level, we assess feasible policy assignment under strict constraints and report cyber-semantic outcomes such as critical-asset protection, attack-impact suppression, service disruption, and defense overhead. This separation between set quality and deployment quality reflects the central practical question of autonomous defense: not only whether a method can discover good trade-offs, but whether it can offer deployable choices for different operational regimes.

Our results show that a constrained Pareto policy-set formulation provides a more realistic basis for autonomous cyber defense than single-policy or purely scalarized baselines. By explicitly modeling deployment trade-offs and evaluating both frontier quality and constrained deployment behavior, we show that autonomous cyber defense is better framed as constrained trade-off management over deployable policy sets than as single-policy reward maximization.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate autonomous cyber defense as a constrained multi-objective sequential decision problem with three operational objectives---security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense overhead---under deployment-relevant operational constraints.
    \item We instantiate a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework in CybORG to construct deployment-aware defense policy sets rather than a single fixed operating point.
    \item We introduce a deployment-aware evaluation protocol that explicitly separates candidate-set quality from final deployment quality, combining Pareto metrics with strict-constraint policy assignment and cyber-semantic outcomes.
    \item We provide empirical evidence that constrained Pareto policy sets yield stronger deployment flexibility and more useful feasible choices than scalarization-based and preference-conditioned baselines for autonomous cyber defense.
\end{itemize}


\section{Threat Model and Deployment Assumptions}
\label{sec:threat-deployment}

We study autonomous blue-team defense in CybORG++~\cite{baillie2020cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}, a cyber-range setting in which a defender must react to attacker progress under partial observability and operational side effects. The purpose of this section is to state the assumptions under which policies are trained, evaluated, and selected for deployment. Our focus is not only whether a blue agent can obtain high reward, but whether it can prevent attacker progress toward critical assets while preserving \emph{Business Continuity}, limiting \emph{Defense Cost}, and maintaining \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}.

\subsection{CybORG++ Enterprise Defense Setting}
\label{subsec:threat-env}

The environment is a finite-horizon enterprise-defense process. At each step, the defender receives a cyber observation, selects a blue-team response action, and the environment advances according to both the blue action and a standardized red process. In the concrete CybORG++ instantiation used in this paper, the environment is Scenario2 with a fixed default \texttt{bline} red process and an episode horizon of $T=100$ steps. This setting exposes the main operational structure needed for our study: attacker lateral movement, enterprise footholds, defensive intervention, business-impacting side effects, and critical-host compromise.

The standardized red process is a deliberate modeling choice. We do not treat the attacker as a co-learning adversary that adapts online to the current blue policy. Instead, the red process defines a controlled attack--defense process under which different blue-team learning methods can be compared fairly. This allows us to isolate whether a method learns a useful \emph{set} of deployable defense policies rather than confounding the evaluation with equilibrium learning or online red--blue adaptation. We consider broader red-side variation as a robustness question, but it is not the primary threat model of this paper.

\subsection{Attacker Progression and Critical Assets}
\label{subsec:threat-attacker}

The attacker seeks to progress through the enterprise network toward high-value assets. We summarize this progression using three semantic phases. First, an \emph{enterprise foothold} occurs when the attacker compromises an enterprise host that can support further lateral movement. Second, the interaction may enter a \emph{pre-critical window}, where the attacker has not yet compromised the critical asset but the current compromise pattern creates elevated risk. Third, a \emph{critical breach} occurs when a critical asset becomes compromised; if the compromise persists across steps, the run enters a persistent-breach regime.

These phases are not intended to reveal privileged information to the deployed blue policy. They are semantic summaries derived from environment-side state for reward construction, candidate diagnostics, and replay-level audit. The deployed policy acts only on defender-visible observations. The phase structure matters because critical compromise is a tail-risk event: a policy that performs well in average return can still be unacceptable if it repeatedly allows the attacker to cross from the pre-critical window into realized critical breach.

\subsection{Defender Observability and Response Space}
\label{subsec:threat-defender}

The blue defender operates under partial observability. At time $t$, the defender observes $o_t$, which summarizes information exposed by CybORG++ such as alerts, host-level telemetry, and defender-visible status. The defender does not observe the full latent cyber state, the attacker's internal plan, or future attacker actions. It must therefore infer risk from incomplete evidence and choose actions whose consequences unfold over multiple steps.

The defender selects one action from the CybORG++ blue action space. These actions include analysis, removal, restoration, decoy placement, and other host-level interventions made available by the environment. Such actions differ in both security effect and operational side effect. For example, a restoration action may remove attacker presence but can disrupt business operation; a decoy action may improve security posture but consumes response capacity and can be costly; conservative actions may reduce immediate disruption but allow an attacker foothold to deepen. We therefore treat actions as deployment-sensitive cyber interventions rather than abstract control moves.

This distinction is central to our formulation. Aggressively suppressing attacker progress may improve \emph{Security Effectiveness} while harming \emph{Business Continuity} or increasing \emph{Defense Cost}. Conversely, minimizing intervention can reduce cost in the short term but may degrade \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. A deployable autonomous defender must therefore manage trade-offs rather than optimize a single scalar objective.

\subsection{Deployment Preferences and Hard Limits}
\label{subsec:threat-deployment-preferences}

We distinguish between deployment preferences and hard deployment limits. A deployment preference specifies how an organization ranks the four objectives: \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. For instance, an organization in active containment may assign more weight to security and critical-asset protection, while a business-sensitive regime may place more weight on continuity and response cost.

Hard limits play a different role. They define behavior that should be rejected regardless of its scalar utility under a particular preference. Examples include excessive business disruption, excessive reliance on high-disruption actions, unacceptable mean constraint violation, or realized compromise of a critical asset. These limits are deployment constraints, not merely soft preferences. A policy may score well under a preference vector and still be excluded from deployment if it violates these limits.

The deployment object in this paper is therefore a policy set rather than a single universal policy. Training constructs an archive of candidate blue policies that occupy different regions of the four-objective trade-off surface. At deployment time, an organization specifies a preference vector and applicable deployment limits; the system then selects a feasible policy from the archive. No policy is retrained during deployment-time assignment.

\subsection{Scope and Non-Goals}
\label{subsec:threat-scope}

Our setting is a controlled CybORG++ cyber-range study of deployment-aware blue-team learning. It is not a full stochastic-game formulation and does not claim equilibrium guarantees against an adaptive learning attacker. The operational constraints are also abstractions of deployment acceptability: they capture business-disruption tolerance, costly-intervention tolerance, and semantic critical-safety guards, but they do not encode organization-specific service-level agreements, approval workflows, legal rules, or human escalation procedures.

The claim supported by this setting is intentionally bounded. We evaluate whether learning a constrained four-objective policy set provides more useful deployable blue-team choices than scalarized or reduced alternatives under a shared CybORG++ attack--defense process. We do not claim that the resulting policies are ready for unsupervised live-network deployment or that they eliminate all cyber risk.

\section{Constrained Multi-Objective Formulation of Blue-Team Defense}
\label{sec:formulation}

We now formalize the preceding deployment setting as a constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning problem. The formulation is designed to preserve cyber-defense semantics while making policy-set learning and deployment-time assignment explicit. The four optimized objectives are \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. Throughout the paper, larger values are preferable in every objective dimension; in particular, the \emph{Defense Cost} objective is implemented as a negative cost return, so a larger value corresponds to lower realized cost.

\subsection{Partially Observable Cyber MOMDP}
\label{subsec:formulation-momdp}

We model CybORG++ blue-team defense as a finite-horizon partially observable multi-objective decision process,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{A}, P, Z, \mathbf{r}, \boldsymbol{\psi}, \gamma, T \rangle .
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal{S}$ is the latent cyber state space, $\mathcal{O}$ is the defender observation space, $\mathcal{A}$ is the blue-team action space, $P$ is the transition kernel induced by the red process and blue action, $Z$ is the observation kernel, $\mathbf{r}$ is a four-dimensional reward vector, $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ denotes semantic rollout summaries used for constraints and audit, $\gamma \in [0,1]$ is the discount factor, and $T$ is the episode horizon.

At each time step,
\begin{equation}
    o_t \sim Z(\cdot \mid s_t), \qquad
    a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid o_t), \qquad
    s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t,a_t).
\end{equation}
A trajectory is denoted by
\begin{equation}
    \tau = (s_0,o_0,a_0,r_0,\ldots,s_T,o_T).
\end{equation}
The policy $\pi(a\mid o)$ is observation-based. Environment-side state information may be used to compute semantic rewards and audit signals, but it is not provided as privileged input to the deployed policy.

\subsection{Four Optimized Objectives}
\label{subsec:formulation-objectives}

The per-step reward vector is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_t =
    \left[
        r^{\mathrm{sec}}_t,
        r^{\mathrm{bus}}_t,
        r^{\mathrm{cost}}_t,
        r^{\mathrm{crit}}_t
    \right]^{\top},
\end{equation}
where the four dimensions correspond to \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. For a policy $\pi$, the expected return vector is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}(\pi)
    = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\mathbf{r}_t\right]
    = \left[
        J^{\mathrm{sec}}(\pi),
        J^{\mathrm{bus}}(\pi),
        J^{\mathrm{cost}}(\pi),
        J^{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi)
    \right]^{\top} .
\end{equation}
The objective semantics are as follows.

\paragraph{Security Effectiveness.}
This objective rewards suppression of attacker progress and recovery from compromise. It is improved when the defender reduces security exposure, prevents new compromise, and recovers compromised hosts. In the CybORG++ implementation, this signal combines the environment reward with semantic exposure and recovery terms so that security return reflects more than a raw scalar reward.

\paragraph{Business Continuity.}
This objective captures the operational continuity of defended services. It penalizes business-weighted exposure, disruption caused by defensive actions, and critical events that affect business-relevant assets. A higher Business Continuity return means that the policy maintains more acceptable business operation while still responding to the attacker.

\paragraph{Defense Cost.}
This objective captures response burden and costly intervention. It penalizes expensive or high-disruption actions such as restoration, removal, decoy deployment, or other actions with nontrivial operational cost. Since the objective is represented as negative cost, a higher Defense Cost return means lower realized defense cost.

\paragraph{Critical-Asset Safety.}
This objective explicitly represents tail-risk behavior around critical assets. It penalizes ever entering a critical breach, persistent critical compromise, early critical compromise, and critical dwell time. This objective is part of the optimized reward vector itself. It is not a post-hoc audit score and not a fifth auxiliary penalty.

At the trajectory level, the same semantics can be summarized abstractly as
\begin{align}
    R^{\mathrm{sec}}(\tau)
        &= -\alpha_1 E_{\mathrm{sec}}(\tau)
           + \alpha_2 N_{\mathrm{rec}}(\tau)
           - \alpha_3 I_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau), \\
    R^{\mathrm{bus}}(\tau)
        &= -\beta_1 E_{\mathrm{bus}}(\tau)
           - \beta_2 D_{\mathrm{act}}(\tau)
           - \beta_3 I_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau), \\
    R^{\mathrm{cost}}(\tau)
        &= -\eta_1 A_{\mathrm{cost}}(\tau), \\
    R^{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
        &= -\lambda_1 B_{\mathrm{ever}}(\tau)
           -\lambda_2 B_{\mathrm{persist}}(\tau)
           -\lambda_3\bigl(1-L_{\mathrm{hit}}(\tau)\bigr)
           -\lambda_4 D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau).
\end{align}
Here, $E_{\mathrm{sec}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{bus}}$ are security and business exposure summaries, $N_{\mathrm{rec}}$ counts recovery from compromise, $I_{\mathrm{crit}}$ counts realized critical-impact events, $D_{\mathrm{act}}$ summarizes business disruption induced by blue actions, $A_{\mathrm{cost}}$ is action operation cost, $B_{\mathrm{ever}}$ indicates whether a critical breach ever occurs, $B_{\mathrm{persist}}$ indicates persistent critical breach, $L_{\mathrm{hit}}$ is a normalized latency score for the first critical hit, and $D_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is critical dwell. The coefficients are nonnegative and fixed by the CybORG++ instantiation. The equations are intended to define the objective semantics; the implementation uses equivalent per-step vector rewards for learning.

\subsection{Deployment Constraints and Semantic Guardrails}
\label{subsec:formulation-constraints}

Preferences express what an organization values, while constraints express what the organization will not deploy. Let $\mathbf{g}(\pi)$ denote deployment-relevant violation summaries, including business-disruption violation, costly-intervention violation, high-disruption action rate, and semantic critical-risk indicators. A policy is feasible under deployment budget $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ if
\begin{equation}
    \pi \in \mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\delta})
    \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
    g_m(\pi) \leq \delta_m, \qquad m=1,\ldots,M .
\end{equation}
In the main CybORG++ deployment evaluation, the primary deployment-facing constraints are business and cost limits. These limits are naturally expressed as lower bounds on the corresponding returns,
\begin{equation}
    J^{\mathrm{bus}}(\pi) \geq d_{\mathrm{bus}},
    \qquad
    J^{\mathrm{cost}}(\pi) \geq d_{\mathrm{cost}},
\end{equation}
where a larger $J^{\mathrm{cost}}$ means lower realized Defense Cost. During policy-set construction and deployment diagnostics, we also track semantic safety guardrails such as final critical compromised hosts, ever-critical breach, persistent critical breach, and critical dwell. These semantic guardrails ensure that a policy is not considered deployable merely because it has good average scalar utility.

This separation avoids conflating objectives and rejection rules. Critical-Asset Safety is one of the four optimized objectives; semantic guardrails are the deployment and audit mechanisms used to check whether the learned behavior actually satisfies the intended safety semantics.

\subsection{Pareto Dominance and Candidate Policy Sets}
\label{subsec:formulation-pareto}

Because the four objectives conflict, a single policy need not dominate all others. We say that policy $\pi'$ dominates policy $\pi$ if
\begin{equation}
    J_i(\pi') \geq J_i(\pi) \quad \text{for all } i\in\{\mathrm{sec},\mathrm{bus},\mathrm{cost},\mathrm{crit}\},
\end{equation}
with strict inequality for at least one objective. A policy is Pareto-optimal within a candidate set if it is not dominated by any other candidate. A learned policy archive $\Pi$ induces a four-dimensional Pareto-front approximation
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(\Pi) = \{\mathbf{J}(\pi): \pi \in \Pi \text{ and } \pi \text{ is nondominated in } \Pi\}.
\end{equation}
The goal of learning is not to obtain one universally best policy, but to construct a finite archive that covers meaningful feasible trade-offs in this four-objective space.

\subsection{Constrained Deployment-Time Assignment}
\label{subsec:formulation-assignment}

Deployment preferences are represented by a weight vector over the four objectives,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{w} \in \Delta_4
    = \left\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}: \sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i=1\right\}.
\end{equation}
Because objective dimensions may have different scales, assignment is performed using a calibrated objective vector $\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)$ fixed across methods within the same evaluation setting. The utility of policy $\pi$ under preference $\mathbf{w}$ is
\begin{equation}
    U(\pi;\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
\end{equation}
Given an archive $\Pi$ and deployment budget $\boldsymbol{\delta}$, the feasible archive is
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})
    = \Pi \cap \mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\delta}).
\end{equation}
If this set is nonempty, the deployed policy is
\begin{equation}
    \pi^{\star}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
    = \arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})} U(\pi;\mathbf{w}).
\end{equation}
If $\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})$ is empty, the assignment is marked infeasible for that preference and deployment regime. For diagnostics, one may report the least-violating candidate, but it is not counted as a feasible deployment. This constrained assignment rule is the deployment counterpart of policy-set learning: preferences choose among acceptable policies; constraints determine which policies are acceptable in the first place.

\section{Learning Deployable Defense Policy Sets}
\label{sec:learning}

We now describe the learning framework used to construct deployable CybORG++ defense policy sets. The method follows a two-stage Pareto policy-set structure: Stage~1 learns a preference-diverse initial archive, and Stage~2 expands the archive through constrained, semantic-safety-aware Pareto extension. This design builds on the C-MORL view that Pareto-front discovery can be improved by first initializing diverse policies and then extending selected policies toward underexplored objective directions~\cite{liu2025efficient}. Our cyber-defense instantiation modifies this idea in two ways: the objective vector is the four-dimensional CybORG++ semantic reward vector, and Stage~2 is constrained by deployment and critical-safety guardrails.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:learning-overview}

The training pipeline has five components. First, CybORG++ transitions are projected into a four-dimensional semantic reward vector over \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}. Second, Stage~1 trains policies under sampled four-objective preferences to obtain a diverse archive. Third, Stage~2 repeatedly selects promising parent policies from the archive and extends them toward individual objective directions while preserving the other objectives. Fourth, candidate children are ranked and accepted using deployment-aware and semantic-safety-aware criteria. Fifth, deployment-time assignment selects a feasible archive policy for each operational preference without retraining.

\subsection{Semantic Reward Projection in CybORG++}
\label{subsec:learning-reward-projection}

Each CybORG++ step produces a raw environment transition and a defender observation. For learning, the environment-side transition is projected into the four reward dimensions defined in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}. The policy itself receives only the observation $o_t$ and action mask exposed to the blue defender. True-state-derived semantic summaries are used by the environment wrapper to construct rewards, constraint diagnostics, and replay-level audit statistics, not as privileged policy input.

The projection is cyber-semantic rather than a direct reuse of a scalar environment reward. Security Effectiveness combines attacker-suppression and recovery information. Business Continuity incorporates business-weighted exposure and action disturbance. Defense Cost measures the operational cost of the selected blue action. Critical-Asset Safety penalizes realized or persistent critical compromise, early critical hit, and critical dwell. The output of this projection is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_t
    = \left[
        r_t^{\mathrm{sec}},
        r_t^{\mathrm{bus}},
        r_t^{\mathrm{cost}},
        r_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
      \right]^{\top}.
\end{equation}
This representation gives the learner direct access to critical-asset safety as an optimized objective while preserving process-level auditability.

\subsection{Stage~1: Preference-Diverse Pareto Initialization}
\label{subsec:learning-stage1}

Stage~1 constructs an initial archive of policies by sampling preference vectors from the four-objective simplex $\Delta_4$. Let $\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\subset\Delta_4$ denote this preference set. For each $\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}$, we train a separate blue policy using vector-valued rollouts and scalarized policy improvement,
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathbf{w}}(\pi)
    = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}
      \left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_t\right].
\end{equation}
The actor--critic model uses a vector critic head, and the rollout buffer stores vector rewards, vector returns, and vector advantages. During PPO-style policy updates, the vector advantage $\mathbf{A}_t$ is scalarized only for the current preference,
\begin{equation}
    A^{\mathbf{w}}_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_t .
\end{equation}
Thus, the policy-gradient update optimizes the selected preference while retaining objective-wise value estimates.

The resulting policies are not treated as permanently preference-conditioned controllers. The preference vector is used to train each initial policy, but once saved, the policy is an archive element with an associated four-objective return vector. Intermediate and final checkpoints are evaluated, stored in a solution buffer, and filtered for nondominated candidates. The output of Stage~1 is an initial archive $\Pi_{\mathrm{init}}$ that covers diverse trade-offs but may still leave gaps in the feasible Pareto frontier.

\subsection{Stage~2: Constraint-Aware Pareto Extension}
\label{subsec:learning-stage2}

Stage~2 improves the archive by extending selected parent policies toward individual objective directions. At each extension round, the current archive is filtered to identify nondominated candidates. Candidate parents are selected using objective-space diversity and deployment-aware signals. Crowd distance prioritizes sparsely covered regions of the Pareto frontier, while support-aware diagnostics prioritize candidates with stronger Business Continuity, lower realized Defense Cost, lower mean violation, lower high-disruption action rate, and better critical-safety behavior.

Given a selected parent policy $\pi_r$ and a target objective $l\in\{\mathrm{sec},\mathrm{bus},\mathrm{cost},\mathrm{crit}\}$, Stage~2 solves a constrained extension problem of the form
\begin{equation}
    \max_{\pi} \; J_l(\pi)
    \quad \text{s.t.} \quad
    J_i(\pi) \geq \beta J_i(\pi_r),\; i\neq l,
    \qquad
    \pi \in \mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\delta}),
\end{equation}
where $\beta\in(0,1]$ controls how much non-target objectives may regress relative to the parent. The first set of constraints prevents extension toward one objective from destroying the other three objectives. The feasibility constraint incorporates deployment limits and semantic guardrails.

We implement this step with an interior-point-style policy optimization objective rather than relying on a second-order constrained policy optimization update. Let $\widehat{J}_i(\pi)$ denote the sampled surrogate estimate of objective $i$ during the update. The extension objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi)
    = \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{clip}}_l(\pi)
      + \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\neq l}
        \log\left(\widehat{J}_i(\pi)-\beta J_i(\pi_r)+\epsilon\right)
      - \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{deploy}}(\pi),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{clip}}_l$ is the PPO-style clipped surrogate for the target objective, $t$ is the barrier parameter, $\epsilon$ is a small numerical stabilizer, and $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{deploy}}$ penalizes or rejects deployment-constraint violations. The log-barrier term discourages movement into regions that degrade non-target objectives below the parent-relative threshold, following the IPO motivation for constrained policy optimization~\cite{achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo,liu2025efficient}.

\subsection{Semantic-Safety-Aware Candidate Acceptance}
\label{subsec:learning-acceptance}

After a child policy is trained for a target objective, it is re-evaluated before being added to the archive. Acceptance is not based solely on target-objective improvement. The candidate must also remain deployable and must not introduce unacceptable critical-risk regression. The diagnostics include Business Continuity return, Defense Cost return, mean constraint violation, high-disruption action rate, final critical compromised hosts, ever-critical breach, persistent critical breach, critical dwell, and critical-hit latency.

This acceptance rule is designed for pre-critical containment. A useful child should improve or diversify the four-objective archive while preserving the ability to stop attacker progression before realized critical breach. The method does not hard-code a single fixed action pattern such as always restoring. Instead, it uses the four-objective reward and semantic guardrails to prefer policies whose rollout behavior remains deployable and whose critical-asset risk does not regress.

When multiple feasible children are available, the archive update ranks them by deployment support, strict feasibility margin, semantic low-risk score, and objective improvement. When a child improves a target objective but worsens deployment acceptability or critical-safety behavior, it can be excluded even if it appears attractive in raw objective space. This is the key difference between unconstrained Pareto expansion and deployable policy-set learning for cyber defense.

\subsection{Deployment-Time Policy Assignment}
\label{subsec:learning-deployment-assignment}

After Stage~2, the learned archive $\Pi$ contains candidate policies and their four-objective return vectors. Deployment-time adaptation is performed by constrained assignment, not by additional training. For a preference $\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_4$ and deployment budget $\boldsymbol{\delta}$, the system first forms $\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})$ and then selects
\begin{equation}
    \pi^{\star}(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
    = \arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\delta})}
      \mathbf{w}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
\end{equation}
This rule is the constrained version of set-max policy assignment: the selected policy maximizes preference utility among deployable candidates. If the feasible archive is empty, the deployment request is reported as infeasible for that preference and regime. This convention prevents an unsafe or overly disruptive policy from being counted as a successful deployment merely because it is the least bad available candidate.

\subsection{Replay-Level Semantic Audit}
\label{subsec:learning-semantic-audit}

The final component is replay-level semantic audit. Although audit metrics are not additional training objectives, they are essential for interpreting whether the learned policy set achieves the intended cyber behavior. We partition rollouts into enterprise foothold, pre-critical window, critical breach, and persistent critical breach. We then report semantic outcomes such as ever-critical-breach rate, persistent-critical-breach rate, mean critical dwell, critical-hit latency, risk-tier distribution, and pre-critical action-family statistics.

This audit separates two claims that would otherwise be conflated. A method may prevent realized critical breach in a controlled CybORG++ evaluation while still allowing near-miss trajectories in which the attacker reaches an enterprise foothold. Such a method should be described as breach-free under the evaluated setting, not risk-free. This distinction is important for deployment because the absence of realized critical compromise does not imply the absence of operational risk.

\subsection{Computational Structure}
\label{subsec:learning-complexity}

The computational structure follows the two-stage C-MORL template. If $n$ is the number of objectives, $N$ is the number of selected extension parents, and $K$ is the number of extension rounds, the Pareto extension component scales as $O(nKN)$ under a fixed per-extension training budget. In our CybORG++ instantiation, $n=4$. This linear dependence on the number of objectives is important because the method optimizes Critical-Asset Safety as a first-class objective rather than collapsing it into a scalar penalty or handling it only after training.

\section{Experimental Testbed and Evaluation Questions}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{CybORG Instantiation}
\label{subsec:setup-environment}

We instantiate the formulation in the CybORG cyber-defense environment~\cite{baillie2020cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}. CybORG provides a partially observable attack--defense process with cyber-specific observations, defender actions, attacker progression, and critical assets. We use the implemented V2.4 protocol in Scenario2 with the default \texttt{bline} red process and an episode horizon of $T=100$ steps.

This setup is intentionally controlled. It is realistic enough to expose lateral movement, enterprise footholds, and critical-host compromise, yet stable enough to support fair cross-method comparison under shared preferences and shared deployment thresholds. Our goal is not equilibrium analysis against a co-learning attacker. Instead, we study whether a defender can learn a \emph{deployable set} of cyber-defense operating points under a standardized attack--defense process.

\subsection{Four-Objective Instantiation}
\label{subsec:setup-objectives}

Our experiments use four defender-side objectives: \emph{security effectiveness}, \emph{business continuity}, \emph{defense cost}, and \emph{critical-asset safety}. Security rewards suppression of attacker progress and reduction of realized impact. Business rewards preservation of service continuity and lower disruption. Cost rewards lower intervention burden and less reliance on expensive actions. The fourth objective, critical-asset safety, explicitly targets tail-risk behavior around enterprise footholds and critical hosts.

This fourth objective is part of the optimization problem itself; it is not an auxiliary post-hoc penalty. The deployment-time semantic audit described below is used to validate whether learned policies exhibit the intended behavior, but it is not a fifth training objective. Operational constraints remain deployment-time limits on business disruption and costly intervention, and the Stage~2 expansion procedure is explicitly aware of these limits during policy-set construction.

\subsection{Compared Methods}
\label{subsec:setup-baselines}

The main-text comparison is intentionally minimal and strictly same-protocol. Across the two main tables, we report six methods that were all executed under the current four-objective protocol: \emph{Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set} (our method), \emph{Stage-1 Policy Archive}, \emph{Weighted-Sum Scalarization}, \emph{Preference-Conditioned PPO}, \emph{Lagrangian PPO}, and \emph{Unconstrained Stage-2 Policy Set}. Table~\ref{tab:set-quality} compares the candidate-set methods \emph{Constraint-Aware Stage-2}, \emph{Stage-1 Archive}, \emph{Weighted-Sum}, and \emph{Preference-Conditioned PPO}. Table~\ref{tab:deployment-quality} compares deployment-time performance for \emph{Constraint-Aware Stage-2}, \emph{Stage-1 Archive}, \emph{Weighted-Sum}, \emph{Lagrangian PPO}, and the \emph{Unconstrained Stage-2} ablation.

\emph{Weighted-Sum Scalarization} is the scalarization baseline. \emph{Stage-1 Policy Archive} measures how far a preference-diverse initialization archive can go without Stage~2 expansion. \emph{Preference-Conditioned PPO} is a conditioned-policy baseline evaluated on the same shared preference grid and summarized here only at the candidate-set level. \emph{Lagrangian PPO} is a deployment-only single-policy constrained baseline that optimizes a security-plus-critical-safety objective under shared business and cost thresholds, rather than constructing a candidate archive. \emph{Unconstrained Stage-2 Policy Set} keeps Stage~2 expansion but removes explicit constraint-aware shaping, letting us isolate the role of constrained expansion. Older baselines such as PCN and single-objective methods are not included in the current main-text comparison because they were not rerun under the same four-objective protocol; mixing them with the current results would not be a fair comparison.

\subsection{Evaluation Questions and Metrics}
\label{subsec:setup-metrics}

Our evaluation has three layers: candidate-set quality in four-dimensional objective space, constrained deployment performance under shared thresholds, and cyber-semantic safety under modeled attack--defense dynamics.

\paragraph{RQ1: Does the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set learn a stronger four-objective candidate set?}
At the set level, we report hypervolume (HV), expected utility (EU), coverage ratio, number of unique assigned policies, and number of retained Pareto records~\cite{hayes2022guide,liu2025efficient}.

\paragraph{RQ2: Does the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set achieve stronger constrained deployment performance?}
At deployment time, we report security return, business return, cost return, feasible rate, mean constraint violation, final critical compromised hosts, and critical-impact count under a shared-threshold assignment protocol.

\paragraph{RQ3: Does constraint-aware Stage~2 change replay-level cyber semantics relative to Unconstrained Stage~2?}
We run a symmetric replay-level semantic audit on the \emph{shared-threshold selected deployed policy} for both Stage~2 variants. Each selected policy is replayed for 20 audit episodes per seed, partitioned into \emph{enterprise foothold}, \emph{pre-critical}, and \emph{critical-present} phases, and summarized with risk tiers, questionable action rules Q2--Q5, pre-critical action-family statistics, and phase-conditioned target-focus summaries.

\paragraph{RQ4: What do Stage~2 expansion and constraint-aware extension contribute?}
We compare the Constraint-Aware Stage-2 Policy Set against the Stage-1 Policy Archive to isolate the value of Stage~2 expansion, and against the Unconstrained Stage-2 Policy Set to isolate the value of explicit constraint-aware shaping during expansion.

All methods are evaluated on the same shared preference grid over the four-objective simplex. For each preference vector, a final deployed policy is selected using the same deployment-time assignment rule and the same shared business/cost thresholds.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:setup-impl}

All main-text results use three seeds $\{7,11,19\}$. The same seeds are used for candidate-set evaluation, constrained deployment evaluation, and semantic replay audit. For policy-set methods, Stage~2 starts from the Stage~1 archive, expands selected parent policies, and evaluates final deployment through the same shared-threshold assignment protocol. For RQ3, we replay only the final selected deployed policy from Table~\ref{tab:deployment-quality} for Constraint-Aware and Unconstrained Stage~2; best-feasible alternates and multi-candidate shortlists are intentionally excluded from the main-text semantic comparison. Detailed hyperparameters, semantic rule definitions, and configuration files are deferred to the artifact package.

Stage~2 solves a discovery problem: it enlarges the archive with stronger candidates that translate into much better deployment viability. Constraint-aware shaping solves an acceptability problem: it steers Stage~2 expansion toward points that remain feasible under the shared thresholds and more disciplined under replay-level semantic audit. Critical-Asset Safety as a fourth objective solves a representation problem: it prevents Stage~2 from selecting operating points that can still look competitive in the older three-objective formulation yet fail catastrophically once deployment is evaluated against current critical-safety requirements.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Defense and Cyber Ranges}
\label{subsec:related-cyber}

Autonomous cyber defense studies sequential blue-team decision making in environments where attacker progress, partial observability, and action side effects all matter. Cyber ranges such as CybORG have made this line of work reproducible by providing standardized defender observations, action spaces, attacker processes, and scenario-driven evaluation settings~\cite{baillie2020cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}. Our work builds on this line, but shifts the emphasis from learning a single strong defense policy to learning a set of deployable blue-team operating points.

\subsection{Security Work on Defensive Trade-offs}
\label{subsec:related-security}

A large body of security research foregrounds operational trade-offs rather than abstract optimization metrics. Classic security-systems papers such as Bohatei frame the problem around practical deployment constraints like scalability, responsiveness, and adversary resilience, with the system and evaluation story carrying the paper before a consolidated late related-work section~\cite{fayaz2015bohatei}. Defensive deception work studies how decoys and psychological signals alter attacker behavior under controlled penetration settings~\cite{fergusonwalter2021deception}. Recent system and planning papers treat attack and defense through concrete security artifacts such as exploit chains, attack paths, and action semantics~\cite{depasquale2024chainreactor}. Other work uses game-theoretic or economic models to reason about how different security incentives or interventions affect aggregate loss~\cite{hastings2025investment}. Work on proactive defenses against LLM-based agents likewise frames the defender's objective in terms of concrete operational outcomes such as delay, detection, and disruption of malicious behavior~\cite{ayzenshteyn2025cloak}. Our paper is aligned with this security-first tradition: the learning machinery is important, but it is subordinate to the question of whether a defender obtains better deployable choices.

\subsection{MORL and Constrained Optimization}
\label{subsec:related-morl}

Multi-objective reinforcement learning studies sequential decision problems with multiple, potentially conflicting objectives~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022guide}. A central distinction is between preference-conditioned policies and explicit policy-set approaches. Conditioned methods encode the preference vector directly into the model~\cite{yang2019generalized,reymond2022pcn,lu2023morl}, while policy-set methods explicitly construct a Pareto archive and assign policies at evaluation time~\cite{liu2025efficient}. Constrained reinforcement learning addresses the orthogonal question of whether high-return policies remain acceptable under safety, cost, or operational budgets~\cite{achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo,paternain2019zeroduality}. Our work combines these views in a cyber-defense setting: we use constrained multi-objective optimization not to train one policy for one preference, but to discover additional feasible blue-team operating points that remain meaningful at deployment time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Results Support}
\label{subsec:discussion-claims}

The empirical evidence supports a specific claim: deployment-aware policy-set learning provides a stronger basis for autonomous cyber defense than scalarized or reduced alternatives when the goal is to support heterogeneous deployment preferences under operational constraints. The evidence now appears at three aligned levels: improved four-objective candidate-set quality, stronger constrained deployment performance, and more disciplined replay-level cyber semantics relative to Unconstrained Stage~2 under a symmetric selected-policy audit.

The paper does \emph{not} support a stronger claim of universal dominance or complete risk elimination. The unconstrained Stage~2 variant can still look better on some raw return dimensions, and in the symmetric RQ3 comparison both selected Stage~2 variants remain dominated by Tier~1 Near-Miss rather than Tier~0 Safe. These boundaries matter because they show that the present contribution is a constraint-aware deployment perspective for blue-team defense, not a complete solution to all cyber-defense decision settings.

\subsection{Limitations and Practical Implications}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

This work has three main limitations. First, the attacker is represented by a standardized process rather than a fully adaptive learning opponent. This is appropriate for isolating blue-team decision quality, but it leaves open the question of how policy-set learning behaves under broader attacker variation. Second, the operational constraints remain proxy-based abstractions of deployment limits. Although they capture disruption tolerance and intervention burden, they do not yet encode organization-specific SLAs, approval workflows, or asset-criticality rules. Third, the current evaluation is centered on CybORG and a fixed family of scenarios.

At the same time, the operational implication of the current results is clear. In blue-team settings, the useful object is often not a single best policy, but a set of feasible responses spanning multiple operating regimes. A defender under high-pressure containment and a defender in a service-sensitive environment may need different acceptable policies. Policy-set learning addresses exactly this need.

A second practical implication concerns interpretability. By tying the four objectives to observable rollout summaries such as final critical compromises, impact events, intervention counts, expensive-action frequency, and critical-safety semantics, the framework makes deployment reasoning more legible than a monolithic scalar reward. Even when the underlying optimization is complex, the resulting operating points can still be discussed in the language of concrete defender outcomes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied autonomous cyber defense as a constrained four-objective blue-team decision problem in which defenders must balance security effectiveness, business continuity, defense cost, and critical-asset safety under explicit deployment limits. We instantiated this view in CybORG with a two-stage learning framework that constructs a deployment-aware set of feasible blue-team policies rather than one fixed response policy.

The resulting evidence is both conceptual and empirical. Deployment-aware policy-set learning improves candidate-set quality, yields stronger constrained deployment performance than scalarized and reduced baselines under a shared protocol, and changes replay-level cyber semantics relative to Unconstrained Stage~2 while remaining breach-free but not risk-free. More broadly, the paper argues that autonomous cyber defense should be treated not only as policy optimization, but as constrained deployment under heterogeneous operational preferences and explicit critical-safety semantics.

\section{Ethical Considerations}

The currently visible Stage~2 trainer uses the following defaults unless overridden by configuration: \texttt{stage2\_top\_k}=2, \texttt{delta\_biz}=12.0, \texttt{delta\_cost}=20.0, and \texttt{lambda\_lr}=0.01. \subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
All methods in the current paper are evaluated on the same four-objective preference grid, under the same shared business and cost thresholds, and with the same final assignment rule for selecting a feasible deployed policy. Policy-set methods are evaluated both at the archive level and at the deployment level. Selected policies are additionally replayed through the semantic audit, which partitions each trajectory into enterprise-foot\-hold, pre-critical, and critical-breach stages before computing risk tiers, questionable-action rules, and pre-critical action-family statistics.
