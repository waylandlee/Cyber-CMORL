\begin{abstract}
Autonomous cyber defense requires sequential decisions under competing operational objectives. Defenders must contain attacker progress while preserving business continuity and limiting the cost and disruption of defensive actions, making the problem inherently constrained and multi-objective. However, most existing learning-based approaches optimize a scalarized objective or produce a single deployable policy, which is insufficient when organizations differ in their tolerance for risk, disruption, and defense overhead. In this paper, we formulate blue-team cyber defense in CybORG as a constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning problem with three objectives: security effect, business impact, and defense cost. We adopt a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework that first learns an initial set of preference-diverse defense policies and then expands the policy set under operational constraints to improve frontier quality and deployment flexibility. We evaluate both candidate-set quality and final deployment quality using Pareto metrics, preference-conditioned utility, and cyber-semantic outcomes. Results show that our approach yields higher-quality policy sets, broader preference coverage, and stronger feasible deployment choices under strict operational constraints than scalarization-based and conditioned-policy baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Autonomous cyber defense is not a single-objective optimization problem. In operational networks, defenders rarely act under a single notion of success. When an attacker is moving laterally toward critical assets, an aggressive response may improve security outcomes but also disrupt essential services, interrupt business workflows, or impose substantial remediation costs. In quieter situations, excessive intervention may be equally undesirable, because frequent isolation, restoration, or host-level actions can degrade availability and burden operators without proportionate security benefit. In practice, blue-team decision making is therefore governed by competing objectives: limiting attacker progress, preserving business continuity, and controlling the operational cost of defense.

These tensions become even sharper in high-pressure cyber scenarios. During ransomware-style propagation, defenders must react quickly enough to contain compromise, yet actions that are effective in stopping spread may also disable revenue-critical hosts or interrupt mission-essential processes. In environments with strict service-level requirements, even a technically effective defense can be operationally unacceptable if it causes excessive collateral disruption. Conversely, overly conservative policies may preserve availability in the short term while allowing attacker footholds to deepen. Such cases illustrate that realistic cyber defense is not simply about maximizing a scalar reward; it is about selecting acceptable trade-offs under heterogeneous operational requirements and deployment constraints.

Recent progress in reinforcement learning has made autonomous cyber defense increasingly tractable in simulated environments. However, most existing learning-based approaches still optimize a scalarized objective or aim to produce a single deployable policy. This is a poor fit for real defensive operations. Different organizations, and even the same organization at different moments, may prioritize security effectiveness, business continuity, and intervention cost differently. A hospital under active disruption, a cloud provider during peak service hours, and an enterprise responding after hours may rationally prefer different defense trade-offs. A single fixed policy is therefore unlikely to serve all deployment settings well. What is needed instead is a deployment-aware policy set that spans meaningful trade-offs and supports policy assignment under changing preferences and operational limits.

This motivates a constrained multi-objective view of autonomous cyber defense. Rather than collapsing all defensive outcomes into a single reward, we model defense as a sequential decision problem with three objectives: security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense cost. We further impose operational constraints to represent unacceptable side effects, such as excessive disruption or overly aggressive intervention. Under this formulation, the goal is not merely to learn one ``best'' policy, but to construct a policy set that approximates the Pareto trade-off frontier and enables flexible deployment under different preferences. This perspective is especially relevant in cyber operations, where acceptable behavior is shaped not only by technical security goals but also by organizational tolerance for disruption and response overhead.

In this paper, we study constrained multi-objective reinforcement learning for blue-team cyber defense in CybORG~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp}. We adopt a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework that first learns an initial set of preference-diverse defense policies and then expands the policy set under operational constraints. Our goal is not to solve a fully general multi-agent game-theoretic equilibrium problem, but to examine whether deployment-aware policy sets can provide a more realistic basis for autonomous defense than single-policy or purely scalarized baselines. To answer this question, we distinguish between two forms of performance: the quality of the learned candidate policy set, and the quality of the final policy deployed under a particular preference.

Accordingly, our empirical study focuses on whether constrained Pareto policy sets improve deployment relevance in cyber defense. At the set level, we evaluate frontier quality, expected utility, and preference coverage. At the deployment level, we examine feasible policy assignment under strict constraints and assess cyber-semantic outcomes such as critical asset protection, attack-impact suppression, and defense overhead. This evaluation design reflects a practical reality: the value of an autonomous defense method lies not only in whether it can produce a strong policy, but also in whether it can provide multiple acceptable policies for different operational regimes.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate autonomous cyber defense as a constrained multi-objective sequential decision problem with security, business, and cost objectives under operational deployment constraints.
    \item We instantiate a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework in CybORG to construct deployment-relevant defense policy sets rather than a single fixed policy.
    \item We introduce a deployment-aware evaluation perspective that separates candidate-set quality from final deployment quality and emphasizes preference coverage and strict-constraint feasible choices.
    \item We provide empirical evidence that Pareto policy sets offer stronger deployment flexibility than scalarization-based and conditioned-policy baselines in autonomous cyber defense.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Defense}
\label{subsec:related-cyber}

Autonomous cyber defense has emerged as an important research direction for learning-based security systems, especially in environments that model persistent attacker--defender interaction under partial observability. In such settings, a blue defender must make sequential response decisions based on incomplete information about compromise state, attacker progress, and the downstream impact of defensive actions. Benchmark platforms such as CybORG have made it possible to study these questions in a standardized and reproducible way by providing cyber-specific observation spaces, defender action sets, attacker processes, and scenario-driven evaluation settings~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp}.

Most prior learning-based cyber-defense work, however, is still centered on optimizing a single reward or a single deployment objective. This treatment is often too restrictive for realistic operations. In practice, defenders must simultaneously consider whether an action slows attacker progress, whether it degrades mission or business availability, and whether it imposes excessive intervention burden on operators or infrastructure. This motivates treating autonomous cyber defense as a multi-objective and constrained sequential decision problem rather than a standard scalar-reward task.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning}
\label{subsec:related-morl}

Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) studies sequential decision making with multiple, potentially conflicting objectives~\cite{roijers2013survey, hayes2022guide}. A central distinction in MORL is between methods that learn a \emph{single preference-conditioned policy} and methods that explicitly learn a \emph{policy set} that approximates a Pareto frontier. Preference-conditioned methods typically incorporate a preference vector into the policy or value function and aim to generalize across user preferences within a single network~\cite{yang2019generalized, reymond2022pcn, lu2023morl}. These methods are attractive because they offer compact representations and direct conditioning at inference time. However, they can struggle when the preference space is broad, when optimization across objectives becomes unstable, or when a single network fails to provide uniformly strong solutions across heterogeneous trade-offs.

An alternative line of work explicitly constructs a policy set that approximates the Pareto frontier~\cite{liu2025efficient}. This view is especially appealing when deployment requires choosing among qualitatively different operating points rather than committing to one universal compromise. A policy-set perspective also makes it natural to reason about coverage, expected utility under varying preferences, and policy assignment at deployment time. For our setting, this is particularly relevant: different organizations, and even the same organization under different operating conditions, may rationally prefer different trade-offs among security effectiveness, business continuity, and response cost. These considerations make Pareto-oriented policy sets a better fit than a single fixed defense policy.

\subsection{Constrained Reinforcement Learning}
\label{subsec:related-crl}

Constrained reinforcement learning addresses sequential decision problems in which high-reward policies may nevertheless be unacceptable because they violate safety, cost, or operational limits~\cite{achiam2017cpo, liu2020ipo, paternain2019zeroduality}. In cyber defense, this perspective is important because many aggressive actions that improve short-term containment can also create excessive disruption, service interruption, or remediation overhead. A constrained formulation therefore provides a principled way to encode deployment boundaries rather than treating all side effects as interchangeable components of a scalar reward.

Recent work has also begun to connect constrained optimization with multi-objective learning. In particular, constrained MORL formulations use optimization of one objective subject to lower bounds or budgets on others, thereby enabling structured exploration of trade-offs rather than relying solely on scalarization~\cite{huang2022constrained, liu2025efficient}. This is closely related to our setting, where operationally acceptable blue-team behavior is shaped by both desired outcomes and hard tolerances on unacceptable side effects. At the same time, most existing constrained RL and constrained MORL work is developed for generic control problems or standard MORL benchmarks, rather than for deployment-aware cyber-defense settings with explicit business and operational semantics.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}
\label{subsec:related-positioning}

Our work lies at the intersection of autonomous cyber defense, multi-objective reinforcement learning, and constrained sequential decision making. We do not aim to solve a fully general game-theoretic equilibrium problem for red--blue cyber conflict, nor do we claim a new universal MORL paradigm. Instead, we study how a constrained, Pareto-oriented policy-set framework can be instantiated in a cyber-defense environment and evaluated from a deployment perspective.

Relative to prior cyber-defense learning work, our focus is not only on learning a strong defense policy, but on constructing a \emph{deployment-relevant policy set} that spans meaningful trade-offs among security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense cost. Relative to preference-conditioned MORL, we emphasize explicit policy-set construction and policy assignment rather than relying on a single conditioned network to represent all deployment preferences. Relative to constrained RL, we center the problem on multi-objective blue-team defense with cyber-specific operational semantics. Finally, beyond standard Pareto and utility metrics, we adopt a deployment-aware evaluation perspective that separates candidate-set quality from final deployment quality and examines feasible choices under strict operational constraints.

This positioning reflects the main goal of the paper: to show that autonomous cyber defense is better treated as a constrained trade-off management problem, and that deployment-aware Pareto policy sets provide a more realistic basis for blue-team decision making than scalarized or single-policy alternatives.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We study blue-team autonomous cyber defense in a partially observable attack--defense environment instantiated in CybORG~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp}. The defender must repeatedly select response actions based on incomplete information about compromise state, attacker progress, and the side effects of defensive interventions. Unlike standard single-reward formulations, our setting requires reasoning about competing operational objectives and explicit deployment limits. We therefore model autonomous cyber defense as a constrained multi-objective sequential decision problem.

\subsection{Cyber Defense Setting}
\label{subsec:problem-setting}

We consider a blue defender interacting with an evolving cyber environment driven by both attacker behavior and defender responses. At each time step, the defender observes a partial view of the network and chooses an action intended to slow attacker progress, protect critical assets, or recover compromised components. The effect of a defensive action is not limited to immediate security outcomes: it may also change future attack opportunities, alter service availability, and incur operational burden. This makes cyber defense inherently sequential and strongly path-dependent.

To capture these characteristics, we model the problem as a constrained multi-objective partially observable decision process
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{A}, P, \Omega, \mathbf{r}, \mathbf{c}, \gamma \rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ is the latent state space, $\mathcal{O}$ is the observation space, $\mathcal{A}$ is the defender action space, $P$ denotes the transition dynamics, $\Omega$ denotes the observation function, $\mathbf{r}$ is a vector-valued reward, $\mathbf{c}$ is a vector of constraint signals, and $\gamma \in [0,1)$ is the discount factor.

\subsection{Observation, Action, and Transition}
\label{subsec:problem-oat}

Let $s_t \in \mathcal{S}$ denote the latent cyber state at time step $t$, including host status, attacker footholds, and other environment variables that are not fully observable to the defender. The defender receives an observation
\begin{equation}
o_t \sim \Omega(\cdot \mid s_t), \qquad o_t \in \mathcal{O},
\end{equation}
and selects a defensive action
\begin{equation}
a_t \in \mathcal{A}.
\end{equation}
The environment then evolves according to
\begin{equation}
s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t),
\end{equation}
where the transition implicitly reflects both defender actions and the attacker process encoded in the environment.

This formulation captures two important properties of realistic cyber defense. First, the defender must act under partial observability rather than from full knowledge of compromise state. Second, actions such as isolation, restoration, or host-level remediation may have delayed and non-local consequences, affecting future security risk as well as business availability. As a result, a policy must be evaluated over long-horizon outcomes rather than immediate action effects alone.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Return}
\label{subsec:problem-return}

Following the multi-objective reinforcement learning perspective~\cite{roijers2013survey, hayes2022guide}, we define each step reward as a three-dimensional vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_t =
\bigl[
r_t^{\text{sec}},
r_t^{\text{biz}},
r_t^{\text{cost}}
\bigr],
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_t^{\text{sec}}$ measures \emph{security effectiveness}, such as slowing attacker progress, reducing attack impact, or protecting critical assets;
    \item $r_t^{\text{biz}}$ measures \emph{business continuity}, capturing how defensive actions affect mission continuity or service availability;
    \item $r_t^{\text{cost}}$ measures \emph{defense cost}, reflecting intervention overhead, operational effort, or response burden.
\end{itemize}

We use a reward convention in which larger values are preferred for all three objectives. Under this convention, lower disruption and lower operational burden correspond to larger business and cost rewards, respectively. For a policy $\pi$, the discounted multi-objective return is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t \mathbf{r}_t
\right].
\end{equation}

This objective design reflects a central feature of autonomous cyber defense: the defender is not optimizing a single notion of success. Policies that aggressively suppress attacker activity may simultaneously create high disruption or intervention cost, while conservative policies may preserve availability at the expense of deeper compromise. A realistic defense policy must therefore manage trade-offs across these competing outcomes.

\subsection{Operational Constraints}
\label{subsec:problem-constraints}

Not all undesirable side effects should be treated merely as soft penalties in a scalar reward. In practical deployments, some behaviors are unacceptable beyond a threshold, regardless of their short-term security benefit. Examples include excessive service disruption, overly frequent high-impact interventions, or remediation patterns that exceed operational tolerance. To capture such boundaries, we introduce constraint signals
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}_t =
\bigl[
c_t^{(1)}, \dots, c_t^{(M)}
\bigr],
\end{equation}
where each component represents a deployment-relevant side effect.

For each constraint dimension $m \in \{1,\dots,M\}$, we require the policy to satisfy
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t c_t^{(m)}
\right]
\leq
\tau^{(m)},
\end{equation}
where $\tau^{(m)}$ is a predefined budget or threshold. This constrained formulation separates \emph{preferences} from \emph{deployment limits}: preferences determine which trade-off is desirable, while constraints determine which behaviors remain operationally acceptable.

\subsection{Preference-Conditioned Utility and Deployment}
\label{subsec:problem-utility}

Although the three objectives are shared across deployments, different organizations or operating conditions may assign different importance to them. We therefore use a preference vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w} \in \Delta^2
\end{equation}
to represent a deployment-specific weighting over security, business, and cost objectives, where $\Delta^2$ denotes the three-objective probability simplex. Given a return vector $\mathbf{J}(\pi)$, the corresponding scalar utility is
\begin{equation}
U(\mathbf{J}(\pi); \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{J}(\pi).
\end{equation}

This deployment view motivates learning a \emph{policy set} rather than a single fixed defense policy. Let $\Pi$ denote a learned set of candidate policies. At deployment time, given preference vector $\mathbf{w}$, the defender selects
\begin{equation}
\pi^{*}(\mathbf{w}) =
\arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi} U(\mathbf{J}(\pi); \mathbf{w}),
\end{equation}
subject to the operational constraints defined above. This formulation follows the policy-set perspective of MORL, in which the goal is to approximate a useful trade-off frontier and support preference-aware policy assignment at test time~\cite{liu2025efficient}.

Overall, our formulation treats autonomous cyber defense as a constrained trade-off management problem. The objective is not to learn one universally optimal policy, but to construct a deployment-relevant policy set that supports different operational preferences while remaining within acceptable deployment boundaries.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We instantiate a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework for constrained multi-objective autonomous cyber defense. The method takes the problem formulation in Section~\ref{sec:problem} and returns a deployment-aware policy set rather than a single fixed defense policy. At a high level, Stage~1 learns an initial set of preference-diverse candidate policies, and Stage~2 expands this set under explicit operational constraints to improve trade-off coverage and deployment relevance. At test time, the final deployed policy is selected from the learned policy set according to a deployment-specific preference vector.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:method-overview}

Let $\mathcal{W}_{\text{init}} \subset \Delta^2$ denote a finite set of training preferences over security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense cost. Our method first trains an initial set of candidate policies
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\text{init}} = \{\pi_{\mathbf{w}} \mid \mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{init}}\},
\end{equation}
where each policy is optimized for a different trade-off preference. This stage provides diverse starting points in objective space rather than a single compromise solution.

Starting from $\Pi_{\text{init}}$, we then perform constrained Pareto-oriented expansion to obtain an enriched policy set $\Pi$. The purpose of this second stage is not simply to improve one scalarized objective, but to discover additional feasible trade-off points that are relevant for deployment. The final output of the method is therefore a policy set
\begin{equation}
\Pi = \mathcal{E}(\Pi_{\text{init}}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{E}(\cdot)$ denotes the constrained expansion and policy-set maintenance procedure.

This design follows the policy-set view of multi-objective reinforcement learning~\cite{liu2025efficient} while adapting it to cyber defense, where different deployments may legitimately prefer different balances among security effectiveness, business continuity, and operational burden.

\subsection{Stage 1: Preference-Diverse Policy Initialization}
\label{subsec:method-stage1}

Stage~1 constructs an initial candidate policy set by training multiple policies under different deployment preferences. For each preference vector $\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}_{\text{init}}$, we optimize the scalarized utility
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathbf{w}}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_t
\right].
\end{equation}
This yields a collection of policies that emphasize different trade-offs among the three objectives.

The role of Stage~1 is not to fully recover the final trade-off frontier. Instead, it provides a diverse set of initialization points from which subsequent constrained expansion can proceed. This is important because a single policy, or a narrow set of similarly trained policies, is unlikely to cover the range of deployment-relevant operating points required in realistic cyber defense. By distributing the initial policies across different preferences, we obtain candidate behaviors that prioritize different combinations of attacker containment, service preservation, and intervention cost.

Practically, each initialized policy is stored together with its estimated return vector and constraint statistics. These estimates provide the basis for the second-stage expansion and later deployment-time assignment.

\subsection{Stage 2: Constrained Pareto Expansion}
\label{subsec:method-stage2}

Although preference-diverse initialization produces multiple candidate policies, it does not by itself guarantee sufficient coverage of high-quality and operationally feasible trade-offs. Stage~2 therefore expands the current policy set under explicit constraints.

Starting from a candidate policy $\pi \in \Pi$, we construct an updated policy $\pi'$ by improving one target objective while preserving the acceptability of the others. At an abstract level, the constrained expansion step can be written as
\begin{equation}
\max_{\pi'} J_{\ell}(\pi')
\quad
\text{s.t.}
\quad
J_{i}(\pi') \geq \kappa_i,\; i \neq \ell,
\quad
C_m(\pi') \leq \tau^{(m)},\; m=1,\dots,M,
\end{equation}
where $J_{\ell}$ denotes the objective currently emphasized for improvement, $\kappa_i$ denotes a lower-bound requirement for the remaining objectives, and $C_m(\pi')$ denotes the expected value of the $m$-th deployment constraint. The thresholds $\tau^{(m)}$ are inherited from the problem formulation and encode operationally unacceptable behavior.

This constrained expansion view is closely related to prior constrained reinforcement learning and constrained MORL formulations~\cite{achiam2017cpo, liu2020ipo, liu2025efficient}, but our emphasis here is deployment relevance in cyber defense. In particular, the purpose of expansion is to discover additional feasible trade-off points rather than to optimize a single final policy. In realistic blue-team settings, this matters because a policy that appears strong along one objective dimension may still be unusable if it creates excessive disruption or remediation burden.

Conceptually, Stage~2 serves two roles. First, it improves frontier quality by moving candidate policies toward previously under-covered regions of the trade-off space. Second, it preserves deployment relevance by explicitly respecting operational boundaries during this search. The result is a candidate policy set that better reflects the feasible choices available to a real defender.

\subsection{Policy Set Maintenance and Deployment-Time Assignment}
\label{subsec:method-assignment}

Throughout training, we maintain a candidate policy set together with estimated objective returns and constraint statistics for each policy. After each expansion round, newly generated policies are inserted into the candidate set, and clearly inferior candidates are removed. In practice, this maintenance step keeps the learned set focused on non-dominated or otherwise deployment-relevant trade-off points rather than allowing the set to be dominated by redundant policies.

At deployment time, a defender does not retrain a new policy. Instead, given a deployment-specific preference vector $\mathbf{w}$, the final policy is selected from the learned candidate set. Let
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\text{feas}}
=
\left\{
\pi \in \Pi \;\middle|\; C_m(\pi) \leq \tau^{(m)},\ \forall m
\right\}
\end{equation}
denote the subset of candidate policies that satisfy the operational constraints. The deployed policy is then chosen as
\begin{equation}
\pi^{*}(\mathbf{w})
=
\arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi_{\text{feas}}}
U(\mathbf{J}(\pi);\mathbf{w}),
\end{equation}
where $U(\mathbf{J}(\pi);\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{J}(\pi)$ is the preference-conditioned utility defined in Section~\ref{subsec:problem-utility}.

This separation between \emph{candidate policy-set construction} and \emph{deployment-time policy assignment} is central to our deployment-aware perspective. A method may appear strong if evaluated only on one selected policy, yet still fail to provide robust choices across different preferences or operational regimes. By explicitly learning and maintaining a policy set, our framework supports flexible deployment under heterogeneous organizational requirements.

\subsection{Discussion of Design Rationale}
\label{subsec:method-rationale}

The proposed design is motivated by three considerations. First, autonomous cyber defense is inherently multi-regime: organizations differ in how they value security effectiveness, business continuity, and intervention burden, and these preferences may also change over time. Learning a policy set is therefore more appropriate than committing to a single fixed policy.

Second, a two-stage design separates diversity generation from constrained refinement. Stage~1 provides broad coverage over coarse trade-offs, while Stage~2 improves deployment relevance by expanding the policy set under explicit operational boundaries. This division of labor is especially useful in cyber defense, where not all trade-off points are equally meaningful from a deployment perspective.

Third, explicit constraints are important because some side effects are not merely undesirable but operationally unacceptable. Encoding such limits directly in the learning process better reflects real defensive decision making than relying solely on a scalar reward to trade all effects against one another.

Overall, our method operationalizes the constrained multi-objective formulation of cyber defense as the construction of a deployment-aware policy set. This framing directly supports the evaluation perspective adopted later in the paper, where we separately analyze candidate-set quality and final deployment quality.

\section{Environment and Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Cyber Environment and Threat Model}
\label{subsec:setup-environment}

We conduct experiments in the CybORG cyber-defense environment~\cite{baillie2020cyborg, emerson2024cyborgpp}, which provides a partially observable attack--defense setting with cyber-specific observations, defender actions, and attacker processes. Our study focuses on the blue-team defense problem: the defender repeatedly selects response actions based on an incomplete view of network state, while the environment evolves under an adversarial red process. This setting captures the core difficulty of autonomous cyber defense, namely that decisions must be made under uncertainty and may have delayed security and operational consequences.

In the current study, we use a fixed defender-side scenario and a standardized red-side process in order to isolate the quality of blue-team decision making. We do not attempt to solve a fully general multi-agent equilibrium problem; rather, we study how well a deployment-aware constrained multi-objective defense method performs under a controlled but realistic attacker process. Each episode has a finite horizon of $T=100$ steps. This setup is sufficient for evaluating long-horizon trade-offs among attacker containment, service preservation, and intervention burden while keeping cross-method comparisons fair.

\subsection{Objectives, Constraints, and Reward Construction}
\label{subsec:setup-objectives}

Our experiments instantiate the three-objective formulation from Section~\ref{sec:problem}: security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense cost. The security objective rewards defender behavior that suppresses attacker progress, reduces realized attack impact, and protects critical assets. The business objective rewards behavior that preserves service continuity and avoids unnecessary disruption caused by defensive actions. The cost objective rewards behavior that limits operational burden, such as excessive intervention frequency or repeated use of high-cost actions.

These three objectives are intentionally not collapsed into a single hand-designed score. Instead, they are treated as separate optimization targets because realistic deployments may value them differently. In particular, a policy that aggressively contains attacker activity may still be undesirable if it causes excessive business disruption or requires sustained high-cost intervention.

Beyond reward design, we model deployment boundaries through explicit operational constraints. Constraint signals capture side effects that are not merely undesirable, but potentially unacceptable in deployment, such as excessive disruption or overuse of high-impact defensive actions. We study both a \emph{loose} constraint regime and a \emph{tight} constraint regime. The loose regime represents settings with moderate operational tolerance, whereas the tight regime represents more restrictive or high-pressure environments in which acceptable intervention space is narrower. This distinction is important because a defense method may appear strong under permissive conditions while offering very limited feasible choices under stricter deployment requirements.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{subsec:setup-baselines}

We compare the proposed method against baselines from three complementary families.

First, we include \emph{scalarization-based} baselines that optimize a single scalar objective derived from the multi-objective reward vector. These baselines represent the common strategy of committing to a fixed trade-off during training and deploying a single resulting policy.

Second, we include \emph{preference-conditioned} baselines, which aim to represent multiple trade-offs within a single conditioned model~\cite{yang2019generalized, reymond2022pcn, lu2023morl}. These baselines are important because they directly target preference adaptation at inference time and therefore provide a strong alternative to explicit policy-set construction.

Third, we include \emph{set-based and constrained} baselines that isolate key components of our framework. In particular, we compare against a Stage-1-only variant without second-stage Pareto expansion, as well as a variant that removes explicit constrained expansion. We also include a constrained single-policy baseline based on standard constrained reinforcement learning principles~\cite{achiam2017cpo, liu2020ipo}. Taken together, these baselines allow us to compare our deployment-aware policy-set approach against single-policy optimization, conditioned-policy learning, and reduced versions of our own framework.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}
\label{subsec:setup-metrics}

Our evaluation protocol is explicitly deployment-aware. Rather than judging a method only by the performance of a single selected policy, we separately evaluate the quality of the learned candidate policy set and the quality of the final deployed policy selected for a given preference.

At the \emph{set level}, we report standard Pareto-oriented and utility-oriented metrics~\cite{hayes2022guide, liu2025efficient}, including hypervolume (HV), expected utility (EU), coverage ratio, the number of unique assigned policies, and the number of retained candidate policies. These metrics assess different aspects of candidate-set quality: frontier quality, preference coverage, and deployment flexibility.

At the \emph{deployment level}, we report objective returns and constraint-related statistics for the final deployed policy, including security return, business return, defense cost return, feasible rate, and mean constraint violation. In addition, we report cyber-semantic outcomes that are more directly interpretable in defensive terms, such as critical asset protection and attack-impact suppression. These deployment-level metrics are necessary because a method may learn a visually strong frontier while still offering poor feasible deployment choices under realistic operational limits.

To evaluate preference sensitivity, we use a shared preference set over the three-objective simplex for all methods. For each preference vector, a final deployed policy is selected according to the rule in Section~\ref{subsec:method-assignment}. To evaluate robustness under operational pressure, we report results under both loose and tight constraint regimes using the same deployment-time assignment protocol. All methods are evaluated using the same reference points, constraint thresholds, and preference sets within each regime.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{subsec:setup-impl}

All methods are trained under a common protocol to ensure fair comparison. We use the same environment configuration, episode horizon, and evaluation procedure across methods whenever applicable. The proposed method is implemented using a common policy-optimization backbone, with separate runs for different initial preferences in Stage~1 and constrained policy-set expansion in Stage~2. Unless otherwise stated, the main reported results use three random seeds $\{7,11,19\}$.

To reduce confounding factors, methods are compared under aligned training budgets and the same deployment-time evaluation grid. For policy-set methods, all candidate policies are evaluated under the same preference and constraint protocol. For conditioned-policy baselines, evaluation is performed on the same preference set used for policy assignment in our method. Detailed hyperparameters, optimizer settings, and reward-coefficient values are deferred to the appendix.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Statistical Reporting}
\label{subsec:setup-repro}

We report aggregate results as mean $\pm$ standard deviation across random seeds. All main tables and figures are produced from a unified evaluation pipeline using shared reference points, shared constraint thresholds, and a common preference grid within each evaluation regime. This design ensures that differences across methods reflect learned policy quality rather than protocol mismatches.

To support reproducibility, we retain environment configurations, training preferences, evaluation preferences, and aggregated result files for all reported experiments. Additional implementation details, sensitivity analyses, and extended result tables are provided in the appendix. In the final submission, we will also prepare an anonymized artifact package containing the configurations and scripts needed to reproduce the main reported results.

\subsection{Ablation}
The main-text ablation set is locked to \texttt{ours\_stage2}, \texttt{stage1\_only}, and \texttt{no\_constraint\_stage2}, with \texttt{weighted\_sum} included only if space permits. 
This keeps the ablation focused on three causal questions: whether Pareto extension matters, whether constraint-aware expansion matters, and whether the method is more than a scalarization baseline in disguise.

The ablations already visible in Tables~\ref{tab:set-quality} and~\ref{tab:deployment-quality} answer two central questions. 
First, comparing \texttt{ours\_stage2} against \texttt{stage1\_only} shows that Pareto-set expansion is materially useful: the full method improves both hypervolume and expected utility over the initial archive. 
Second, comparing constrained and unconstrained Stage~2 behavior shows that removing constraint awareness can improve some deployment metrics or tight-feasible counts, but at the cost of changing the structure of the set and the interpretation of deployment behavior. For this specific constrained-versus-unconstrained comparison, the fair causal reading should be taken from the locked \texttt{fair\_compare} line, where \texttt{ours\_stage2\_fair} and \texttt{no\_constraint\_stage2\_fair} share the same Stage~2 budget and differ only in \texttt{extension\_mode}. By contrast, the broader main-paper Table~\ref{tab:deployment-quality} is retained as a deployment comparison table rather than as a strictly matched ablation.

This leaves a clear message for the final paper version. 
Pareto extension matters, and constraint awareness matters, but they help in different ways. 
The former improves set quality directly, while the latter shapes which parts of the set remain acceptable under operational limits. 
Additional appendix ablations can extend this with alternate preference distributions and attacker settings, but the main-text causal story is already visible in the current core comparisons.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{From Set Quality to Deployment Value}
\label{subsec:discussion-set-to-deploy}

A central message of this paper is that autonomous cyber defense should not be evaluated only through the lens of a single selected policy. In realistic deployments, the quality of the underlying candidate policy set matters because it determines whether a defender can adapt to different operational preferences without retraining. This is why we explicitly separate candidate-set quality from final deployment quality. A method may achieve reasonable performance for one deployed policy while still providing weak coverage over meaningful trade-offs, poor preference sensitivity, or very limited feasible choices under operational limits.

Our results suggest that the proposed framework is most valuable precisely at the candidate-set level. The method improves frontier quality and expected utility while preserving competitive deployment behavior under constraints. This supports the interpretation that policy-set quality is not merely a descriptive property of the learning process, but part of the method's practical deployment value.

\subsection{Why Deployment-Aware Policy Sets Matter in Cyber Defense}
\label{subsec:discussion-why-sets}

The need for deployment-aware policy sets is particularly strong in cyber defense. Different organizations may rationally prefer different trade-offs among attacker containment, business continuity, and intervention burden. Even within the same organization, these preferences may change across operating conditions: a defender responding during a high-impact incident may accept stronger disruption for faster containment, whereas a service-sensitive setting may prioritize continuity and conservative intervention. A single fixed policy is therefore unlikely to be suitable across all deployment regimes.

Our formulation and evaluation are designed around this operational reality. Rather than treating cyber defense as the search for one universally best policy, we treat it as a constrained trade-off management problem in which different deployment preferences require different acceptable policies. From this perspective, a useful defense method is one that provides a structured set of feasible options, not just one policy that happens to score well under a particular scalarization.

\subsection{What the Results Support and What They Do Not}
\label{subsec:discussion-claims}

The empirical evidence in this paper supports a specific claim: deployment-aware Pareto policy-set learning provides a stronger basis for autonomous cyber defense than scalarized or single-policy alternatives when the objective is to support heterogeneous deployment preferences under operational constraints. The results support this claim at three levels: improved candidate-set quality, structured preference-aware assignment behavior, and competitive final deployment behavior under the common constrained protocol.

At the same time, our results should not be interpreted as showing universal dominance on every metric or under every operating regime. In particular, the strictest tight-constraint regime remains a stress case rather than a completed success story. Likewise, the current study does not solve a fully general red--blue equilibrium problem, nor does it establish robustness across all possible attacker families. We view these boundaries as important: the present contribution is a deployment-aware constrained MORL formulation and evaluation framework for autonomous blue-team defense, not a complete solution to all cyber-defense decision settings.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

This work has several limitations. First, the attacker side is represented through a standardized process rather than a fully adaptive learning opponent. This is appropriate for isolating blue-team decision quality, but it leaves open the question of how deployment-aware policy sets behave under stronger attacker variation. Second, our operational constraints are still proxy-based abstractions of deployment limits. Although they capture important notions such as disruption tolerance and intervention burden, they do not yet represent the full richness of enterprise policy, service-level agreements, or human-in-the-loop procedures.

Third, the current evaluation is centered on CybORG and a fixed family of experimental settings. Broader validation across attacker shifts, scenario shifts, or additional cyber-defense environments would strengthen the generality of the conclusions. Finally, the present paper emphasizes deployment-aware policy-set learning rather than more aggressive algorithmic refinement of the second-stage expansion process. Future work can therefore proceed along two complementary directions: improving the operational realism of the cyber-defense formulation, and improving the efficiency and robustness of policy-set discovery itself.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studied autonomous cyber defense as a constrained multi-objective sequential decision problem in which defenders must balance security effectiveness, business continuity, and defense cost under explicit deployment limits. We instantiated this view in CybORG using a two-stage Pareto-oriented learning framework that constructs a deployment-aware candidate policy set rather than a single fixed defense policy.

Our results indicate that this perspective is useful both conceptually and empirically. The proposed framework improves candidate-set quality, supports more structured preference-aware policy assignment, and maintains competitive deployment behavior under constraints. More broadly, the paper argues that autonomous cyber defense should be treated not only as a policy optimization problem, but as a constrained deployment problem with heterogeneous operational preferences. In that setting, deployment-aware Pareto policy sets provide a more realistic foundation for autonomous blue-team decision making.
