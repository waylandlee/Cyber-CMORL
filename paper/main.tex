LLM-enabled cyber attacks pose a realistic threat in cyberspace, and the autonomous cyber defense (ACD) is a promising technology for confronting AI-enabled attacks.
However, deep reinforcement learning (DRL)-based decision-making for ACD in preventing lateral-movement and responding ransomware in IT\&OT scenarios is a time-sensitive sequential decision problem.
Existing methods typically collapse the problem into a scalarized reward or a single operating point, which ignored the disruptions of critical services, exhaustion of defending resource, and rising critical risks in hindering sophisticated attackers.

This paper presents a constrained multi-objective DRL method for ACD agents by formulating the defender with four deployable objectives: \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}.
Instead of maximizing a single reward policy, our method constructs a deployable policy set through cyber-semantic objectives and constraint-aware Pareto expansion, which different operational preferences can be realized without re-training.
We propose a two-stage Pareto-oriented framework that initializes preference-diverse policies and expands them via constraint-aware Pareto optimization to yield a deployable policy set for ACD.
By implemented in the CybORG++ simulators, our method achieves \textbf{$3.9\times$} higher hypervolume than weighted-sum scalarization, raises expected utility from $-161.57$ to $-41.92$, increases feasible deployment rate to $0.64$, and reduces mean constraint violation from $93.54$ to $0.09$, without critical compromised hosts and critical-impact events.
These experiments show that a constrained trade-off management over multi-objective policy sets can enable the ACD agent with more deployable guarantees than straightforward single-policy reward maximization.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The modern enterprise network often suffers from APT attacks, in which attackers with an initial foothold conduct discovery, execute compromises, make lateral movements, and eventually disrupt business or operational processes.
AI-driven ATP attacks pose a realistic threat with the raising reasoning capabilities of LLMs.
Autonomous cyber defense (ACD), as an AI-powered defense technology, is increasingly being studied as a way to shorten defenders' response loops confronting sophisticated attacks.
Prior works on ACD have emphasized deep reinforcement learning (DRL) as the decision-making mechanism in a partially observable simulator for training an agent ~\cite{vyas2023automated,palmer2023drlsurvey,standen2021cyborg}.
However, an ACD agent requires effective and deployable guarantees in enterprise networks with critical IT and OT services.
Defensive actions are operational interventions that disrupt services, consume resources, or create unacceptable exposure around critical assets.
In IT/OT-adjacent environments, this distinction is especially important because operational technology security must account for performance, reliability, and safety requirements in addition to ordinary cybersecurity objectives~\cite{nist_sp80082r3}.

Existing learning-based ACD methods are not well aligned with this deployment requirement.
Scalarized DRL reduces security effectiveness, service continuity, response overhead, and critical-asset risk into a single reward, which can hide unacceptable behavior behind a high average return.
Preference-conditioned MORL methods can adapt to different objective weights, but they still tend to represent deployment preferences as soft trade-offs rather than strict operational limits~\cite{vanmoffaert2013scalarized,yang2019generalized,basaklar2022pdmorl,lu2023morl,roijers2013survey,hayes2022practical}.
Constrained RL provides mechanisms for optimizing under safety or cost constraints, but standard constrained formulations usually return one operating policy for one specified constraint setting~\cite{altman2021cmdp,achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo,paternain2019duality}.
For ACD, these limitations are consequential. Different phases may require different trade-offs between effectiveness and deployments, and strict limits on business disruption, defense cost, or critical-asset compromise must reject unacceptable policies regardless of scalar utility.
Therefore, ACD requires not only multi-objective optimization, but also constrained learning methods and policy assignment under explicit operational limits.

We formulate ACD as a constrained multi-objective decision-making problem.
The defender operates under partial observability and selects defensive responses, while the attacker is modeled in the ATT\&CK framework.
The ACD agent is optimized over 4 objectives: \emph{Security Effectiveness}, \emph{Business Continuity}, \emph{Defense Cost}, and \emph{Critical-Asset Safety}.
The strategy is not the optimal defense policy, but an evaluated candidate defense policies with a constrained assignment rule.
% In this formulation, preferences specify how to rank acceptable trade-offs, whereas strict operational limits specify behavior that must be rejected regardless of scalar utility.
% Given an operational preference, the agent selects a policy only from the acceptable Pareto set. if no candidate satisfies the specified limits, the assignment is marked infeasible rather than forcing a high-utility but unacceptable policy.

Inspired by policy-set multi-objective reinforcement learning, including recent two-stage Pareto-front discovery methods~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022practical,liu2025cmorl}, we propose \emph{Defense-Aware Constrained Policy-Set Learning} (DA-CPSL). 
DA-CPSL learns constrained multi-objective strategies in two stages. 
Stage~1 trains preference-diverse initial policies over diverse objectives. 
Stage~2 extends selected candidate policies toward individual objective directions while preserving other objectives and enforcing operational constraints. 
Each candidate is evaluated not only by objective returns, but also by operational violations and semantic filters. In assignments, DA-CPSL selects a policy from the acceptable Pareto set under a preference. 
If no candidate satisfies the specified limits, the assignment is marked infeasible rather than forcing a high-utility but unacceptable policy.

The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Formulating the ACD as a constrained multi-objective problem, the model separates trade-offs of objective, constraints of acceptance, and assignments of preference-conditioned to yield deployable guarantees.
    \item Introducing semantics of security as the first-class objective for critical-asset

\subsection{Problem Setting}
\label{subsec:operational-setting}

The Stuxnet and cyber-attacks on the Ukrainian power grid have shown that confrontations in cyberspace are becoming more.
Deploying ACD for a typical enterprise network with IT/OT zones shown in Figure~\ref{fig:cyborg-topology}, we model the scenario including ordinary clients, business-relevant servers, and high-value OT assets, where adversarial actions will make unacceptable impact~\cite{nist_sp80082r3}. 
Therefore, we mainly focus on the cyber-defend in the IT/OT environment rather than physical-process control.

This setting is inherently multi-objective. A defensive response that improves security may interrupt a business service, consume scarce response capacity, or introduce operational side effects if applied too aggressively.
Conversely, delaying intervention may preserve short-term continuity and reduce immediate response burden, but it may allow long-term persistence and exploitation to critical assets.
Therefore, the defender cannot be characterized by average rewards simply, it must manage diverse competing objectives under strict constraints with operational acceptance.

The main problem is the decision-making for ACD in the typical enterprise network with constrained multi-objectives. 
The ACD agent can precept partial-observable information and generate optimal strategies under diverse preferences and strict limits.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{subsec:threat-model}

\paragraph{Adversary.}
The adversary is modeled by the ATT\&CK framework, which obtains an initial foothold in the enterprise network and attempts to compromise high-value or critical assets in IT/OT subnets.~\cite{vyas2023automated,palmer2023drlsurvey}.
% 20260427 三个阶段的名字修改完毕了，暂时先用这3个名字
We model the whole attacking process into 3 phases. 
In the \emph{foothold-establishing} phase, the attacker has compromised non-critical assets in the user-subnet to support further lateral movements.
In the \emph{pre-critical compromising} phase, some assets in the enterprise subnet have been compromised, and the compromising process creates exposures of critical-asset in the operation-subnet.
In the \emph{critical-breaching} phase, at least one critical asset in the operational subnet is compromised.

The establishment of a foothold can be interpreted as the outcome of an upstream \emph{Initial Access} (TA0001) technique such as \emph{Phishing} (T1566).
Then an attacker performs \emph{Remote System Discovery} (T1018) and \emph{Network Service Discovery} (T1046) to identify reachable hosts and exposed vulnerable services, uses \emph{Exploitation of Remote Services} (T1210) and \emph{Privilege Escalation} (TA0004) to expand control, pivots from the user subnet into enterprise servers, and attempts to reach the operational subnet by \emph{Lateral Movement} (TA0008).
The ultimate goal is conducting \emph{Exfiltration} (TA0010) and \emph{Impact} (TA0040) to highly confidential data and high-value asset~\cite{mitre_attack_enterprise}.

\paragraph{Defender.}
In the AICA framework~\cite{kott2023aica}, the ACD is an agent that repeatedly perceives the environment, identifies the current situation, selects a response action, executes actions through available interfaces, and updates its knowledge for future decisions~\cite{kott2023aica}. 

At time \(t\), the ACD agent perceives a partial observation \(o_t\) derived from alerts, logs, and other security-relevant information. 
The ACD agent cannot observe the whole status of environments, including the true compromise status of every host, the current privilege level, the attacking plan, or future exploitations. 
Therefore, the defender can be modeled as a finite-horizon partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), where a strategy maps an observation history \(h_t=(o_0,a_0,\ldots,o_t)\) to a defensive action \(a_t\)~\cite{standen2021cyborg,kiely2023cage2}. 

In Figure~\ref{fig:cyborg-topology}, blue dashed lines denotes the ACD agent's observations and actions across subnet.  
As the red trajectory progresses from a user-side foothold toward enterprise servers and the critical operational asset, the ACD agent can select different interventions, such as monitoring or analyzing a suspicious host, deploying decoy services as honeypots, removing malicious code, restoring a compromised host or server from a clean backup, or enforcing containment by updating firewall rules at subnet boundaries. 
These choices are sequential decisions rather than independent responses. 
For example, analyzing a host preserves service continuity but delays remediation; deploying a decoy can slow exploitation and lateral movement but consumes hardware resources; adding firewall rules can protect the operational subnet but may interrupt legitimate business traffic; and restoration can clear a deeply compromised host but causes stronger service disruption. 
Thus, different defense policies correspond to different ways of trading containment speed, operational disruption, response burden, and critical-asset protection along the same attacker progression.

\paragraph{Critical assets and security semantics.}

In an enterprise network with IT/OT-converged operators, critical assets include systems that support remote access, engineering workflows, historical data, and other high-value services. And these systems use cyber entities, such as PCs, servers, PDAs, and other digital platforms, to provide services, which contains potential cybersecurity vulnerabilities.

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) denotes the set of cyber entities, \(\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) denotes the set of critical-asset set, and \(C_t\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) denotes the set of compromised entities at time \(t\). Then the indicator of critical-breaches can be denoted as:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
:=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } C_t\cap\mathcal{K}\neq\varnothing,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:threat-critical-breach}
\end{equation}
For compactness, we use \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) to denote the indicator function in the following definitions. 
Similarly, we define the pre-critical exposure indicator as
\begin{equation}
b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
C_t\cap\mathcal{K}=\varnothing
\;\land\;
\rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})>0
\right],
\label{eq:threat-precritical}
\end{equation}
The condition \(\rho(C_t,\mathcal K)>0\) indicates that at least one currently compromised non-critical entity creates a potential exposure to a critical asset. 
Thus, \(b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}=1\) denotes a current pre-critical exposure state, whereas \(b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1\) denotes an already realized critical-asset compromise. 
The condition \(C_t\cap\mathcal K=\varnothing\) ensures that the two indicators are mutually exclusive at each time step.

These indicators are not additional observations used for decision-making. 
Meanwhile, they are used to define security semantics for generalized safety of critical assets under cyber-attacks, and evaluate whether a strategy with high-score in average rewards can exhibit unacceptable risks in critical assets. Detailed definitions are formalized in Section~\ref{sec:formulation} and Appendix~\ref{app:critical-audit}.

And we formulated the model by a constrained multi-objective partially observable Markov decision process (CMO-POMDP) in a typical enterprise network, and can be denoted as:

\subsection{Multiple Deployable Objectives}
\label{subsec:objectives}
To ensure both effectiveness and deployment, the single reward function is decomposed into 4 deployable objectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Security Effectiveness} describes the generalized ability to protect the system from adversaries.
    \item \emph{Business Continuity} captures the preservation of services under defensive interventions. 
    \item \emph{Defense Cost} records the burden made by costly or disruptive actions. 
    \item \emph{Critical-Asset Safety} demonstrates the risky behavior around the critical-assets, which focuses on initial contacts, long-term persistence, and fatal impacts.
\end{itemize}

At each time step \(t=0,\ldots,T-1\), we define the reward \(\mathbf{r}_{t}\) as a 4-dimensional vector to include those objectives, which can be denoted as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_t
=
\left[
r_t^{\mathrm{sec}},
r_t^{\mathrm{bus}},
r_t^{\mathrm{cost}},
r_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
\right]^\top ,
\label{eq:reward-vector}
\end{equation}
where \(r_t^{\mathrm{sec}}\), \(r_t^{\mathrm{bus}}\),
\(r_t^{\mathrm{cost}}\), and \(r_t^{\mathrm{crit}}\) correspond to
Security Effectiveness, Business Continuity, Defense Cost, and
Critical-Asset Safety, respectively. For a strategy \(\pi\), the
objective-return vector is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\gamma^t
\mathbf{r}_t
\right]
=
\left[
J^{\mathrm{sec}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{bus}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{cost}}(\pi),
J^{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi)
\right]^\top .
\label{eq:return-vector}
\end{equation}
where, all four objectives \(J^{\mathrm{*}}(\pi)\) are maximization in generations of optimal strategies. In particular, \(J^{\mathrm{cost}}\) is a reformatted \emph{Defense Cost objective}, where high-rewards correspond to a lower response burden.

Using the critical-breach indicator \(b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}\) and the
pre-critical exposure indicator \(b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}\) defined in
Section~\ref{subsec:threat-model}, we introduce two additional
step-level indicators for Critical-Asset Safety. The first-hit indicator is defined as:
\begin{equation}
f_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1
\;\wedge\;
\sum_{k=0}^{t-1} b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}=0
\label{eq:first-critical-hit}
\right].
\end{equation}
%第一次出现 indicator 时已使用 cases 解释清楚；后面在350行用 1[⋅]进行了定义，后面统一使用避免冗余
% 20260428 好的，已看到，这样挺简洁的。另外，注意4个indicator的名称要统一到第一次出现的名称
At \(t=0\), the \(\sum_{k=0}^{t-1} b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}\) is set to \emph{0} to make the  definition valid.
For a persistence horizon \(L\), the indicator of long-term persistence is defined as:
\begin{equation}
p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[
t\geq L-1
\;\wedge\;
\sum_{k=t-L+1}^{t}
b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
L
\right].
\label{eq:persistent-step}
\end{equation}

Then the reward of \emph{Critical-Asset Safety} at each step can be revised as:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
=
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{pre}} b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{hit}} f_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{dwell}} b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{persist}} p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}},
\label{eq:crit-reward}
\end{equation}
where all coefficients \(\lambda_{\mathrm{*}} \geq 0\). And this objective encourages ACD agents to execute protective actions in the pre-critical window before fatal compromise occurred.
For trajectory-level analysis, we define the statistical indicator denoted as:
\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\mathbb{1}
\!\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}>0
\right],
\qquad
D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
=
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
b_t^{\mathrm{crit}},
\label{eq:crit-audit-summary}
\end{equation}
% where indicate whether a critical breach ever occurs and how long critical compromise persists.
% Additional audit summaries, including first critical-hit time and persistent breach rate, are defined in Appendix~\ref{app:critical-audit} and reported in the experimental protocol.
% These trajectory-level variables are used for semantic audit and event-style constraints, not as privileged policy inputs.
Here, \(B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\) indicates whether a critical breach
ever occurs, and \(D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\) records the duration of
critical compromise. Additional semantic critical-risk analysis variables,
including first critical-hit time and persistent breach rate, are defined
in Appendix~\ref{app:critical-audit} and reported in the experimental
protocol. These trajectory-level variables are used for semantic
critical-risk analysis and event-style constraints, not as privileged
policy inputs.

\subsection{Operational Constraints}
\label{subsec:constraints}

In a typical enterprise network, the operational preferences are different from strict constraints.
A preference specifies how an ACD agent ranks trade-offs among multiple objectives, whereas a strict constraint specifies behaviors that must be rejected regardless of utility.

Let \(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}} \subseteq \mathcal{\Pi}\) denote a finite set of candidate strategies. 
For a strategy \(\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}\), we define the \(m\)-th operational violation as:
\begin{equation}
g_m(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi}
\left[
c_m(\tau)
\right],
\qquad
m=1,\ldots,M ,
\label{eq:constraint-expectation}
\end{equation}
where \(c_m(\tau)\geq 0\) is a trajectory-level violation function. 
For constraints calculated by the number of events, \(c_m(\tau)\) can be described as an indicator \(B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)\), then \(g_m(\pi)\) is ratio of events occurred. 
Other constraints can be calculated by summarizing the disruption of services, excessive response burdens, or costly interventions.

For operational constraints \(\boldsymbol{\delta}=[\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_M]^\top\), we define the deployable
candidate set as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\left\{
\pi \in \Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}:
g_m(\pi)\leq \delta_m,
\; m=1,\ldots,M
\right\}.
\label{eq:acceptable-archive}
\end{equation}
Also, the mean constraint violation can be denoted as:
\begin{equation}
V(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\left[
g_m(\pi)-\delta_m
\right]_+ ,
\label{eq:mean-violation}
\end{equation}
where \([x]_+=\max(x,0)\).

With operational constraints, a strategy achieved high-reward will still be rejected if violates limits by exhibiting unacceptable behaviors.

\subsection{Pareto Optimality and Constrained Assignment}
\label{subsec:acceptable-pareto-assignment}

In CMO-POMDP, a strategy that simultaneously optimizes all objectives does not exist. This holds whenever either of the two objectives is not fully parallel to each other. 
In our model, those 4 objectives have conflicts, and a strategy of ACD agent can not be optimized under all operational regimes.
We use Pareto dominance to describe objective-space trade-offs~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022practical}.
Let
\[
\mathcal I=
\{
\mathrm{sec},
\mathrm{bus},
\mathrm{cost},
\mathrm{crit}
\}
\]
denote the objective index set. For two policies \(\pi_a\) and
\(\pi_b\), policy \(\pi_a\) dominates \(\pi_b\) in objective returns,
denoted by \(\pi_a\succ_J\pi_b\), if:
\begin{equation}
\pi_a \succ_J \pi_b
\iff
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&J^{i}(\pi_a)\ge J^{i}(\pi_b),
&& \forall i\in\mathcal I,\\
&J^{j}(\pi_a)>J^{j}(\pi_b),
&& \exists j\in\mathcal I .
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{eq:pareto-dominance}
\end{equation}
and the inequality is strict for at least one objective.
Since all objectives are in maximization form, this dominance relation has a consistent direction.

We define the raw Pareto set induced by \(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}\) as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_P=\left\{\pi \in \Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}: \nexists\,\pi' \in \Pi_{\mathrm{cad}} \setminus\{\pi\}
\ \text{such that}\ \pi'\succ_J \pi\right\}.
\label{eq:raw-pareto-set}
\end{equation}
Raw Pareto optimality is not sufficient for operational deployment. A
non-dominated strategy may still violate service-disruption,
response-burden, or critical-risk limits.

The deployable Pareto set is obtained by applying non-dominated filtering
within the deployable candidate set:
% \begin{equation}
% \Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
% (\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
% =
% \left\{
% \pi\in
% \Pi_{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta}):
% \nexists\,
% \pi'\in
% \Pi_{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
% \setminus\{\pi\}
% \text{ such that }
% \pi'\succ_J \pi
% \right\}.
% \label{eq:acceptable-pareto-set}
% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\bigl\{
\pi\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
:\;&
\nexists\,
\pi'\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
\setminus\{\pi\}
\\
&\mathrm{s.t.}\ 
\pi'\succ_J \pi
\bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:acceptable-pareto-set}
\end{equation}
The deployability filter is applied before the Pareto filter. 

Let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}
=
\left[
w_{\mathrm{sec}},
w_{\mathrm{bus}},
w_{\mathrm{cost}},
w_{\mathrm{crit}}
\right]^{\top}
\in \Delta_4=
\left\{
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{4}:
\sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i=1
\right\}
\label{eq:preference-simplex}
\end{equation}
denote an operator preference over the four objectives. The preference
ranks candidate strategies by scalar utility over the objective-return
vector:
\begin{equation}
U_{\mathbf{w}}(\pi)
=
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\mathbf{J}(\pi).
\label{eq:calibrated-objective}
%\label{eq:preference-utility}
\end{equation}

Given \(\mathbf{w}\), \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), and
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}\), the constrained assignment rule is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Assign}
(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta};\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}})
=
\begin{cases}
\bot,
&
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\emptyset,
\\[4pt]
\operatorname*{arg\,max}\limits_{\pi\in
\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})}
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{J}(\pi),
&
\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:constrained-assignment}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\bot\) denotes infeasibility under the current candidate set and
the specified operational limits.

This constrained assignment is an operational counterpart of policy-set assignment in multi-objective reinforcement learning~\cite{vanmoffaert2014sets,zahavy2021policies,liu2025cmorl}.
Preferences make selecting strategies among deployable sets, while strict constraints determine which strategy is deployable in the first place.


\section{DA-CPSL: Learning Constrained Defense Policy Sets}
\label{sec:learning}

As formulated in Section~\ref{sec:formulation}, the calculation of Pareto optimality can be solved by a learning procedure that builds a policy set rather than optimizing a single policy under a scalarized reward. 
We propose the \emph{Defense-Aware Constrained Policy-Set Learning} (DA-CPSL) method, which generates a set of candidate strategies and assigns a strategy through the constrained assignment rule with deployable preference.

% DA-CPSL follows a two-stage structure inspired by policy-set MORL~\cite{vanmoffaert2014sets,hayes2022practical}, and C-MORL~\cite{liu2025cmorl}. In this structure, diverse initial policies are first trained and selected policies are extended along different objective directions. Each candidate is evaluated by its objective returns, operational violations, and semantic critical-risk analysis. The final assignment is then restricted to the deployable Pareto set. The workflow of DA-CPSL is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dacpsl-overview}.
% We adapt this structure to autonomous cyber defense rather than using it as a generic MORL routine.
% Each candidate is evaluated by its objective returns, operational
% violation summaries, and semantic critical-risk analysis variables. The
% final assignment is restricted to the deployable Pareto set.

DA-CPSL follows a two-stage policy-set learning pattern inspired by
policy-set MORL~\cite{vanmoffaert2014sets,hayes2022practical}, and in
particular by C-MORL~\cite{liu2025cmorl}. In this pattern, diverse
initial policies are first trained, and selected policies are then
extended along different objective directions. We adapt this structure to
autonomous cyber defense rather than using it as a generic MORL routine.
Each candidate is evaluated by its objective returns, operational
violation summaries, and semantic critical-risk analysis variables. The
final assignment is restricted to the deployable Pareto set.

\left\{
x(\pi):
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}
\right\}
\label{eq:evaluated-record-set}
\end{equation}
denote the evaluated record set. The record set separates policy
execution from deployment checking. The policy \(\pi\) acts on the
defender's observation history. The violation summaries and semantic
analysis variables are computed after complete trajectories, and are used
for parent selection, acceptance filtering, and deployment-time
assignment. 


DA-CPSL recomputes several views from the evaluated record set. The raw
candidate set contains all policies in \(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}\). The raw
Pareto set is obtained by applying the dominance relation in
Eq.~\ref{eq:pareto-dominance} to empirical objective-return estimates.
The deployable candidate set contains policies that pass the operational
filter under the specified limits. The deployable Pareto set is then
computed by applying non-dominated filtering within the deployable
candidate set. This ordering is important: a policy that is dominated in
the raw candidate set may still be Pareto-efficient in the deployable
set if the policies that dominate it violate the operational limits.
Operational assignment is performed only on the deployable Pareto set
through Eq.~\ref{eq:constrained-assignment}.


\subsection{Stage 1: Preference-Diverse Initialization}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-stage1}

Stage~1 constructs an initial policy set for Pareto extension.
Let \( \mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\subset\Delta_4 \)
denote the sampled preference set over the four deployable objectives: Security Effectiveness, Business Continuity, Defense Cost, and Critical-Asset Safety. 
For each \(\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}\), DA-CPSL trains one policy \(\pi_{\mathbf{w}}\) by maximizing the scalarized return \cite{vanmoffaert2013scalarized,yang2019generalized}:
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathbf{w}}(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi}
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\gamma^t
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_t
\right].
\label{eq:stage1-scalarized-return}
\end{equation}

The sampled preference is used to guide the initialization. 
It is not a deployment-time condition and is not associated with the trained policy.
After training, \(\pi_{\mathbf{w}}\) is treated as a preference-independent candidate and can be evaluated under any subsequent operator preference. 
Each initial policy is evaluated according to a fixed evaluation protocol to estimate \(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})\), \(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})\), and
\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})\), and is stored as the record \(x(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})\). The result of the initial policy set can be denoted as:
\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{init}}
=
\left\{
\pi_{\mathbf{w}}:
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{init}}
\right\}.
\]

This initialization provides diverse starting points for the second stage.
A policy trained under a sampled preference may still violate service-disruption, response-burden, or critical-risk limits. It becomes eligible for operational assignment only after passing the acceptance filter.

\subsection{Stage 2: Acceptance-Aware Pareto Extension}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-stage2}

Stage~2 extends the candidate policy set from selected parent policies.
Let
\[
\mathcal{I}
=
\{
\mathrm{sec},
\mathrm{bus},
\mathrm{cost},
\mathrm{crit}
\}
\]
denote the objective index set. Given a parent policy \(\pi_r\) and a
target objective \(\ell\in\mathcal{I}\), DA-CPSL updates the parent
toward objective \(\ell\), while constraining the remaining objectives
and the operational limits. This follows the Pareto-extension principle
of optimizing one objective without seriously hampering the other
objectives, but makes both parent selection and post-extension acceptance
depend on deployability.

\subsubsection{Parent Policy Selection}
\label{subsubsec:parent-policy-selection}

At the beginning of each extension round, DA-CPSL recomputes the raw and deployable Pareto sets from the current archive.
We denote \(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}(\Pi)\) for the raw Pareto set and \(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\) for the deployable Pareto set under operational limits \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\).
The parent source set is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}
=
\begin{cases}
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta}),
&
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
\neq
\emptyset,
\\
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}),
&
\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:parent-source-set}
\end{equation}
Thus, DA-CPSL first attempts to extend policies that are already deployable and Pareto-efficient.
If no such policy exists, which may happen in early training, the method falls back to the raw Pareto set to continue exploration.
This fallback only provides training parents and it does not make the selected policy eligible for operational assignment.

% Within \(\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}\), DA-CPSL selects parent policies to improve coverage of the calibrated objective space.
% Extreme candidates for each objective are retained when available.
% The remaining slots are assigned according to a crowd-distance score, so that policies in sparse regions of the current Pareto set are more likely to be extended \cite{deb2002nsga2,liu2025cmorl}.
% When candidates have comparable coverage scores, DA-CPSL prefers lower empirical violation \(V(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\) and lower critical-risk analysis summaries.
% The detailed scoring rule is given in Appendix~\ref{app:parent-selection}.
Within \(\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}\), DA-CPSL selects parent policies to improve
coverage in the objective-return space. Extreme candidates for each
objective are retained when available. The remaining slots are assigned
according to a crowd-distance-style score, so that policies in sparse
regions of the current Pareto set are more likely to be extended
\cite{deb2002nsga2,liu2025cmorl}. The crowding-distance score applies
per-objective range normalization internally. When candidates have
comparable coverage scores, DA-CPSL prefers lower empirical violation
\(V(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})\) and lower semantic critical-risk analysis
summaries. The detailed scoring rule is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:parent-selection}.

\subsubsection{Constrained Pareto Extension}
\label{subsubsec:constrained-pareto-extension}


For a parent policy \(\pi_r\) and a target objective \(\ell\), DA-CPSL sets parent-relative preservation
thresholds for non-target objectives:
\begin{equation}
d_i(\pi_r;\eta_i)
=
\eta_i
J_i(\pi_r),
\qquad
i\in\mathcal{I},\; i\neq \ell ,
\label{eq:parent-relative-threshold}
\end{equation}

where \(\eta_i>0\) is a return-preservation coefficient. In
implementation, \(J_i(\pi_r)\) is replaced by the empirical estimate
\(\widehat{J}_i(\pi_r)\). The threshold is set on the same stored return
scale as the objective-return vectors used for Pareto filtering and
assignment. Since cyber-defense objectives are stored as signed returns,
\(\eta_i\) should be interpreted as a threshold coefficient rather than a
universal percentage-loss parameter.

The idealized extension problem can be formulated as follows:
\begin{align}
\pi_{\ell}^{+}
\in
\arg\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{\Pi}}
&\quad
J_{\ell}(\pi)
\label{eq:ideal-extension}
\\
\mathrm{s.t.}
&\quad
J_{i}(\pi)
\geq
d_i(\pi_r;\eta_i),
\qquad
i\in\mathcal{I},\; i\neq \ell ,
\nonumber\\
&\quad
g_m(\pi)
\leq
\delta_m,
\qquad
m=1,\ldots,M .
\nonumber
\end{align}
The first constraint prevents the target objective from being improved by collapsing the remaining objectives.
The second constraint prevents the deployable policy from exceeding the specified operational limits.

In practice, Eq.~\ref{eq:ideal-extension} is optimized with a PPO-style surrogate that combines target-objective improvement, return-preservation terms, and violation-penalty terms \cite{schulman2017ppo,achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo}.
The surrogate is used to generate a candidate policy, and does not certify deployability.

After the update, the child policy is evaluated under the fixed
evaluation protocol to obtain \(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}\),
\(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\), and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\). The
post-update record is then passed to the acceptance filter and
candidate-set set update in Section~\ref{subsec:dacpsl-archive-update}.

The complete surrogate objective and workflow are given in Appendix~\ref{app:dacpsl-details}.

\subsubsection{Post-extension Evaluation and Acceptance Filtering}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-archive-update}

The extension surrogate only produces a child candidate. A candidate is
not certified for deployment merely because it improves a target
objective or enriches the raw Pareto set. After each extension update,
DA-CPSL evaluates the child policy \(\pi_{\ell}^{+}\) under the fixed
evaluation protocol and computes objective returns, operational violation
summaries, and semantic critical-risk analysis variables:
\begin{equation}
x(\pi_{\ell}^{+})
=
\left(
\pi_{\ell}^{+},
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi_{\ell}^{+}),
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi_{\ell}^{+}),
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi_{\ell}^{+})
\right).
\label{eq:child-record}
\end{equation}
The candidate policy set and the evaluated record set are then updated as:
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}
\leftarrow
\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}
\cup
\{\pi_{\ell}^{+}\},
\qquad
\mathcal{X}
\leftarrow
\mathcal{X}
\cup
\{x(\pi_{\ell}^{+})\}.
\label{eq:archive-update}
\end{equation}

Then, DA-CPSL recomputes candidate-set views from the updated record set.
The raw candidate set contains all evaluated candidates in
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}\). The raw Pareto set is obtained by applying the
dominance relation in Eq.~\ref{eq:pareto-dominance} to this full
candidate set using empirical objective-return estimates. This set is
used to monitor the quality and coverage of the learned candidate set,
and it also preserves policies for subsequent extensions.

The deployable set is computed separately by the empirical counterpart in Eq.~\ref{eq:acceptable-archive} as follows:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\left\{
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}}:
\widehat{g}_m(\pi)\leq \delta_m,
\; m=1,\ldots,M
\right\}.
\label{eq:empirical-deployable-set}
\end{equation}
The deployable Pareto set is then obtained by applying non-dominated filtering within this deployable set.
% \begin{equation}
% \widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
% (\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
% =
% \left\{
% \pi\in
% \widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{dep}}
% (\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta}):
% \nexists\,
% \pi'\in
% \widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{dep}}
% (\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})\setminus\{\pi\}
% \text{ such that }
% \pi'\succ_{\widehat{J}}\pi
% \right\},
% \label{eq:empirical-deployable-pareto-set}
% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\bigl\{
\pi\in
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
:\;&
\nexists\,
\pi'\in
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
\setminus\{\pi\}
\\
&\mathrm{s.t.}\
\pi'\succ_{\widehat{J}}\pi
\bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:empirical-deployable-pareto-set}
\end{equation}
% where \(\succ_{\widehat{J}}\) denotes the dominance relation computed by empirical objective estimates.
% This ordering is important: a policy that is dominated in the raw archive may still become Pareto-efficient
% within the deployable set if the policy that dominates it violates the operational limits.
where \(\succ_{\widehat{J}}\) denotes Pareto dominance computed from the
empirical objective-return estimates. Since deployability is checked
before non-dominated filtering, a policy dominated in the raw candidate
view may still belong to the deployable Pareto view when the policies
dominating it violate the operational limits.

The semantic critical-risk analysis is stored in \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)\).
These variables identify unsafe trajectories that may not be visible from average rewards.
They are used by the acceptance filter and tie-breaking of parent-selection, while operational assignment is restricted to the deployable Pareto set.
If this set is empty, DA-CPSL reports infeasibility rather than assigning a policy that violates the specified limits.

\subsection{Deployment-time Policy Assignment}
\label{subsec:dacpsl-operational-assignment}

After the candidate policy set has been constructed, DA-CPSL applies the
constrained assignment rule in Eq.~\ref{eq:constrained-assignment} to the
learned finite set. Given an operator preference
\(\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_4\) and operational limits
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), DA-CPSL first recomputes the deployable Pareto
set
\[
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
\]
from the stored objective-return estimates, violation summaries, and
semantic critical-risk analysis variables.

% Given an operator preference \(\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_4\) and operational limits \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), DA-CPSL first applies the operational filter and recomputes the deployable Pareto set
% \(
% \widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
% (\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta}).
% \)
% % The Pareto filter is applied after the deployability filter. Therefore, a candidate that is dominated in the raw archive can still be selected for assignment if the policies dominating it violate the operational limits.
% The Pareto filter is applied after the deployability filter. Therefore, a candidate
% that is dominated in the raw candidate set can still be selected for
% assignment if the policies dominating it violate the operational limits.

% If the deployable Pareto set is empty, DA-CPSL does not force a policy to be chosen. DA-CPSL assigns the policy with the largest calibrated utility within the deployable Pareto set:
% \begin{equation}
% \Pi^\star(\mathbf{w},\delta;\Pi_P)
% =
% \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi\in \Pi_P^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi_P,\delta)}
% \mathbf{w}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
% \label{eq:constrained-assignment}
% \end{equation}
If the deployable Pareto set is empty, DA-CPSL does not force a policy
choice:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Assign}_{\mathrm{DA\mbox{-}CPSL}}
(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\bot,
\qquad
\text{if }
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})
=
\emptyset .
\label{eq:dacpsl-infeasible-assignment}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\bot\) denotes infeasibility under the learned candidate set and
the specified operational limits.

Otherwise, DA-CPSL assigns a policy with the largest empirical utility in
the deployable Pareto set:
\begin{equation}
\pi_{\mathrm{DA\mbox{-}CPSL}}^{\star}
(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
\in
\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi\in
\widehat{\Pi}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}
(\Pi_{\mathrm{cad}},\boldsymbol{\delta})}
\mathbf{w}^{\top}
\widehat{J}(\pi).
\label{eq:dacpsl-operational-assignment}
\end{equation}

The assignment step does not require retraining or online policy adaptation.
Changing \(\mathbf{w}\) only changes the utility ranking in Eq.~\ref{eq:dacpsl-operational-assignment}. Changing \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) only recomputes the deployability filter from the stored violation summaries and semantic critical-risk analysis variables.
Thus, the learned archive can support different operator preferences and operational limits while preventing high-utility but non-deployable policies from being assigned.


\section{Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation-setup}

This section evaluates our method in a controlled cyber-range simulator
for ACD agents. The goal of the evaluation is not to show that an
autonomous defender is ready for unsupervised production operation.
Instead, we test whether DA-CPSL constructs a stronger candidate policy
set, whether its selected policies satisfy shared operational limits, and
whether the selected policies exhibit safer replay-level critical-risk
behavior under the evaluated protocol.

\subsection{Evaluation Questions}
\label{subsec:eval-questions}

We organize the evaluation around four research questions.

\paragraph{RQ1: Candidate-set quality.}
Does DA-CPSL construct a stronger four-objective candidate policy set
than scalarized or non-acceptance-aware alternatives? This question
evaluates objective-space candidate-set quality using policy-set metrics
such as hypervolume, expected utility, coverage ratio, and the number of
distinct assigned policies.

\paragraph{RQ2: Operational deployability.}
Does DA-CPSL produce more deployable selected policies under shared
operational limits? This question evaluates feasible rate, mean
constraint violation, and the realized security, business,
response-burden, and critical-asset outcomes of selected policies.

\paragraph{RQ3: Replay-level cyber semantics.}
Do selected DA-CPSL policies contain attacker progression before it
crosses from pre-critical exposure into realized critical breach? This
question evaluates trajectory-level semantic analysis metrics, including
ever-critical-breach rate, persistent-critical-breach rate, critical
dwell, first critical-hit time, and pre-critical response behavior.

\paragraph{RQ4: Component necessity.}
Which components are necessary for the observed behavior?
This question tests whether Stage~2 extension, acceptance-aware shaping,
and Critical-Asset Safety are required for both candidate-set quality and
operational deployability.

\subsection{Cyber-Range and Objective Instantiation}
\label{subsec:eval-cyber-range}

We instantiate the threat model in the CybORG++ Scenario2~\cite{standen2021cyborg,emerson2024cyborgpp}
as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cyborg-topology}. The instantiation
provides partial observations, host-level response actions,
post-compromise attacker progression, business-impacting defensive side
effects, and critical-asset compromise semantics.

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments use the standard fixed baseline
attacker process, a finite horizon of \(T=100\), vector observations, and
the same Scenario2 configuration. Choosing the fixed attacker process
lets us compare different decision-making methods under the same
attack-defense process. Four objectives in the cyber range correspond to
the formulation in Section~\ref{subsec:objectives}: Security
Effectiveness, Business Continuity, Defense Cost, and Critical-Asset
Safety. Critical-Asset Safety is optimized as the fourth objective and is
also evaluated through semantic replay analysis summaries. The

\subsection{Baseline Methods}
\label{subsec:eval-methods}

We compare DA-CPSL with six baselines and ablations under the
same four-objective protocol and shared deployment thresholds.
The compared methods are:
\emph{Stage-1 Candidate Set},
\emph{Weighted-Sum PPO},
\emph{Preference-Conditioned PPO},
\emph{Lagrangian PPO},
\emph{Unconstrained Stage~2}, and
\emph{Three-Objective DA-CPSL}.

These methods isolate different failure modes of deployment-aware
autonomous defense. Weighted-Sum PPO tests whether separately trained
scalarized policies are sufficient. Preference-Conditioned PPO tests
whether one preference-conditioned policy can cover the four-objective
trade-off surface. Lagrangian PPO tests whether a single constrained
policy can satisfy shared operational limits. Stage-1 Candidate Set
isolates the initialization stage. Unconstrained Stage~2 removes
acceptance-aware shaping from Pareto extension. Three-Objective
DA-CPSL removes Critical-Asset Safety and tests whether the critical-risk
objective is necessary.

All methods are evaluated using the same objective definitions,
deployment thresholds, and replay-level semantic analysis rules whenever
applicable. Implementation details and baseline-specific protocol notes
are given in Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-details}.


\subsection{Metrics and Assignment Protocol}
\label{subsec:eval-metrics}

The evaluation follows the three-layer structure of
Section~\ref{sec:formulation}: objective-space candidate-set quality,
operational deployability, and replay-level cyber semantics.

For candidate-set quality, we report hypervolume, expected utility,
coverage ratio, the number of distinct assigned policies, and the number
of retained Pareto records. Hypervolume and expected utility measure the
quality of the empirical Pareto front under the shared four-objective
protocol; they do not by themselves imply operational deployability.

For operational deployability, buffer-based methods first select one
representative policy from the empirical Pareto front using a
threshold-aware selector. The selector prefers policies satisfying the
shared business and cost thresholds, and then breaks ties by smaller
violation and higher objective returns. We report feasible rate, mean
constraint violation, selected objective returns, final critical
compromised hosts, and critical-impact count.

For replay-level semantic analysis, we replay the selected policy under
the fixed evaluation protocol and report trajectory-level critical-risk
behavior, including ever-critical breach, persistent critical breach,
critical dwell, first critical-hit time, near-miss tiers, and
phase-conditioned response behavior. These semantic metrics are
diagnostic: zero observed critical breach under the replay protocol is
interpreted as evidence of pre-critical containment, not as risk
elimination.

Detailed metric definitions, assignment rules, and finite-sample
interpretation of zero-observed critical events are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:metric-details}.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:eval-reproducibility}

All main experiments are run with seeds \(7\), \(11\), and \(19\). The
Stage~2 candidate set is initialized from the corresponding Stage~1
candidate set for the same seed. Candidate-set evaluation, deployment
evaluation, and semantic analysis use the same cyber-range
instantiation.

The implementation stores each candidate as an evaluated record including
objective estimates, operational violation estimates, and semantic
analysis summaries; the record structure and artifact contents are
described in Appendices~\ref{app:candidate-records}
and~\ref{app:artifact-notes}. Additional implementation details,
semantic rule definitions, per-seed outputs, and semantic-analysis
vectors are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:implementation-notes},
\ref{app:semantic-rules}, \ref{app:per-seed-main-results},
and~\ref{app:additional-semantic-analysis}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Defense and Cyber Ranges}

Autonomous cyber defense has emerged as a research direction for using
learning agents to detect, contain, and respond to attacker behavior in
networked environments. Surveys of automated and reinforcement-learning
based cyber defense emphasize that the field requires both realistic
cyber environments and decision-making agents capable of operating under
partial observability, large action spaces, and adversarial dynamics
~\cite{vyas2023automated,palmer2023drlsurvey,thompson2024entity,palmer2025egta}. CybORG introduced a gym
for autonomous cyber agents, and subsequent work has used CybORG and
CAGE-style challenge settings to study learned network defenders
~\cite{standen2021cyborg,foley2022autonomous}. CybORG++ extends this
line with improved tooling and faster experimentation for enterprise
network defense research~\cite{emerson2024cyborgpp}.

Most cyber-range ACD work evaluates a trained defender by episodic
reward, action success, or scenario-specific measures. Foundational
studies have examined tabular learning and deep RL defenders in cyber
ranges~\cite{applebaum2022bridging,foley2022autonomous}, while reward
shaping work has shown that the training signal strongly affects learned
defense behavior~\cite{bates2023reward}. Our work uses the same general
cyber-range tradition but addresses a different decision problem. Rather
than training one defense policy for a single scalar reward, DA-CPSL
learns an evaluated archive of defense policies and performs constrained
assignment under operational preferences and strict limits.

\subsection{Defensive Trade-offs and Operational Constraints}

Cyber defense actions often have operational side effects. Restoring a
host may remove attacker presence but disrupt services; deception may
slow attacker progression but consume response resources; conservative
response may preserve short-term continuity while increasing exposure
around critical assets. This motivates treating autonomous defense as a
decision problem with competing objectives rather than as pure attack
suppression~\cite{odriscoll2024morlcyber}. In critical-service or IT/OT-adjacent environments, this
trade-off is particularly important because OT security guidance
emphasizes safety, reliability, and continuity alongside cybersecurity
risk reduction~\cite{nist_sp80082r3}.

Existing ACD and cyber-range studies recognize operationally meaningful
metrics, but they usually do not formalize the defender's decision object
as a policy archive with strict acceptance constraints. Our formulation
separates preferences from strict limits: preferences rank acceptable
trade-offs, while operational constraints reject unacceptable behavior
regardless of scalar utility. This distinction is central to DA-CPSL and
to our evaluation, where objective-space quality, operational
assignment, and replay-level semantic audit are reported separately.

\subsection{Safe, Constrained, and Assured Learning}

Constrained reinforcement learning studies policies that maximize return
while satisfying expected cost or safety constraints. Constrained MDPs
provide the classical formulation~\cite{altman2021cmdp,dutta2021constraints}, and policy
optimization methods such as CPO and interior-point policy optimization
provide algorithmic mechanisms for constraint-aware learning
~\cite{schulman2015trpo,achiam2017cpo,liu2020ipo,tessler2018rcpo,xu2021crpo,paternain2019duality}. These methods are closely related to
our Stage~2 extension problem, where a policy improves one objective
while preserving other objective values and satisfying operational
limits.

Our setting differs from conventional constrained RL in two ways. First,
we do not train a single policy for one constrained objective. DA-CPSL
constructs a policy archive so that different operator preferences can be
handled by assignment rather than retraining. Second, our constraints
include replay-level critical-risk semantics and empirical operational
acceptance. We therefore do not claim formal safety guarantees. Instead,
we treat acceptance as an empirical filter under the stated evaluation
protocol and report semantic audit evidence to expose critical-risk
behavior that may be hidden by average return.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning and Policy-Set Methods}

Multi-objective reinforcement learning addresses settings where multiple
objectives must be optimized jointly and decision makers may have
different preferences over those objectives~\cite{roijers2013survey,hayes2022practical,parisi2016pareto,lu2023morl}.
One common approach trains a single preference-conditioned policy that
takes the preference vector as input~\cite{yang2019generalized,basaklar2022pdmorl}. Another
approach trains or maintains a finite set of policies to approximate a
Pareto set, enabling later policy assignment for a given preference
~\cite{vanmoffaert2014sets,xu2020prediction,alegre2022optimistic,alegre2023gpi,felten2024morld,ropke2024divide,zahavy2021policies}.
Policy-set methods are attractive for autonomous defense because they
allow the defender to adapt to changing operational preferences without
retraining a new policy for every preference.

C-MORL is particularly relevant because it uses a two-stage policy-set
learning procedure: preference-diverse initialization followed by
Pareto-front extension, where one objective is improved while the others
are constrained~\cite{liu2025cmorl,huang2022constrainedmorl,laumanns2006epsilon}. It then performs policy assignment
from the learned Pareto set. DA-CPSL adopts this policy-set learning
philosophy, but changes the problem object and assignment rule for
autonomous defense. Each candidate policy is evaluated by objective
returns, operational violation summaries, and semantic audit variables;
final assignment is performed from the acceptable Pareto set; and the
assignment may return infeasible when no candidate satisfies the strict
limits. Thus, DA-CPSL is not only a MORL archive-construction method
applied to a cyber range. It is a defense-aware, acceptance-aware, and
audit-aware policy-set learning framework for autonomous cyber defense
under operational constraints.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper formulated autonomous cyber defense as a constrained multi-objective decision-making problem rather than a single scalar-reward policy-learning problem.
We argued that deployable defense requires separating objective trade-offs, strict acceptance constraints, and preference-conditioned policy assignment.
To capture critical-risk behavior around high-value assets, we introduced Critical-Asset Safety as a first-class objective and used replay-level semantic analysis as a diagnostic mechanism.

We proposed DA-CPSL, a defense-aware constrained policy-set learning method that constructs an evaluated archive of candidate defense policies.
DA-CPSL combines preference-diverse initialization, acceptance-aware Pareto extension, semantic candidate analysis, and constrained assignment over the acceptable Pareto set.
This design allows operator preferences to select among acceptable policies while preventing preference weights from overriding strict operational limits; when no candidate satisfies the limits, DA-CPSL reports infeasibility rather than forcing an unacceptable policy choice.

Evaluations show that DA-CPSL improves both objective-space archive quality and operational assignment acceptability.
Across the evaluated seeds, it achieves stronger hypervolume and expected utility than scalarized and non-acceptance-aware alternatives, increases feasible-assignment rate, and substantially reduces mean constraint violation.
Replay-level semantic analysis further shows no observed critical breach or persistent critical breach for the selected DA-CPSL policy under the fixed evaluation protocol, while near-miss trajectories remain visible.
These results support a bounded interpretation: DA-CPSL improves pre-critical containment under the evaluated threat model and cyber-range protocol, but it does not eliminate cyber risk.

The ablations further show that Stage~2 extension improves archive discovery, acceptance-aware shaping improves operational acceptability, and Critical-Asset Safety changes the representation of acceptable defense trade-offs.
These findings suggest that ACD should be evaluated not only by average reward or raw Pareto quality, but also by whether selected policies satisfy operational limits and exhibit acceptable security semantics.

Several limitations remain.
Our evaluation uses a controlled cyber-range instantiation with a fixed attacker process for fair comparison, so the reported acceptance and breach-free results are empirical under this environment and should not be interpreted as formal safety guarantees or robustness to all attacker variants.
Future work should extend DA-CPSL-style policy archives to broader attacker variation, richer incident-response workflows, human approval and escalation processes, and complementary formal or runtime assurance mechanisms for learned defense policies.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{app:ethical-considerations}

This work studies autonomous cyber defense in a controlled cyber-range
setting. It does not conduct experiments on production networks, does
not use real user traffic, and does not collect or process personal data.
The study is limited to simulated attack--defense interaction and
replay-level analysis of learned defense policies.

\paragraph{Potential benefits.}
The intended benefit of this work is to improve the evaluation and
deployment readiness of autonomous cyber defense agents. In particular,
the paper argues that learned defenders should not be evaluated only by
average reward or raw Pareto-front quality. Instead, selected policies
should also be checked against operational limits and critical-asset
semantics. This framing can help reduce the risk of deploying learned
defense policies that appear effective in aggregate metrics but cause
unacceptable service disruption, response burden, or critical-asset
exposure.

\paragraph{Dual-use considerations.}
Autonomous cyber defense research is inherently dual-use because it
models attacker progression, defender response, and network compromise
states. To reduce misuse risk, this paper focuses on defensive decision
making and does not introduce new vulnerability-discovery techniques,
real exploit chains, credential-stealing procedures, or instructions for
compromising real systems. The attacker behavior used in the experiments
is confined to a cyber-range scenario and is described only at the level
needed to define the threat model and reproduce the evaluation.

\paragraph{Safety of autonomous response.}
The proposed method is not intended for unsupervised deployment in
production networks. Defensive actions such as host restoration, decoy
deployment, or containment can have operational side effects. For this
reason, the paper treats deployment as constrained policy selection
rather than unrestricted reward maximization. If no candidate policy
satisfies the specified operational limits, DA-CPSL reports infeasibility
instead of forcing an unacceptable action choice. In a real deployment,
such infeasibility should trigger human review, escalation, or fallback
to established incident-response procedures.

\paragraph{Limitations of the evidence.}
The reported results are empirical under a controlled cyber-range
protocol with finite replay episodes and a fixed attacker process. Zero
observed critical breach in replay should not be interpreted as a formal
safety guarantee or as evidence that critical compromise cannot occur.
The paper explicitly reports finite-sample interpretations and discusses
attacker-shift limitations to avoid overstating the security claim.

\paragraph{Artifact release.}
The artifact release will be limited to materials necessary for
reproducing the controlled experiments. It will not include production
network data, real incident logs, credentials, or weaponized exploit
code. Any released attacker scripts or scenario configurations are
included only to reproduce the cyber-range evaluation and should not be
used against real systems.

\section{Formal Definitions and Empirical Acceptance}
\label{app:formal-acceptance}

This appendix gives the formal definitions, algorithmic details, and
supplementary experimental evidence used by the main paper. We use
\emph{archive} to denote the evaluated candidate policy set maintained by
DA-CPSL. Each archived policy stores objective estimates, operational
violation estimates, and replay-level semantic analysis summaries.

\subsection{Critical-Risk Analysis Variables}
\label{app:critical-audit}

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) denote the set of cyber entities, and let
\(\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) denote the set of critical assets.
For an evaluated trajectory, let \(C_t\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) be the set
of compromised entities at timestep \(t\). We use bracketed indicator
notation throughout the appendix, consistent with the main text:
\(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) equals one when the condition inside the brackets
is true and zero otherwise.

The step-level critical-breach indicator is
\begin{equation}
 b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 C_t\cap\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset
 \right].
 \label{eq:app-critical-breach}
\end{equation}
The enterprise-foothold indicator is
\begin{equation}
 b_t^{\mathrm{foot}}
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 C_t\setminus\mathcal{K}\neq\emptyset
 \right].
 \label{eq:app-enterprise-foothold}
\end{equation}
The pre-critical exposure indicator is
\begin{equation}
 b_t^{\mathrm{pre}}
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 C_t\cap\mathcal{K}=\emptyset
 \;\wedge\;
 \rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})>0
 \right],
 \label{eq:app-precritical}
\end{equation}
where \(\rho(C_t,\mathcal{K})\) is a policy-independent analysis function
that marks elevated exposure around critical assets. It is used for
evaluation and replay-level semantic analysis only; it is not a
privileged input to the defense policy.

The first critical-hit indicator is
\begin{equation}
 f_t^{\mathrm{crit}}
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1
 \;\wedge\;
 \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}=0
 \right].
 \label{eq:app-first-critical}
\end{equation}
We use the convention that an empty sum is zero, so the definition also
applies at \(t=0\). For a persistence horizon \(L\), the step-level
persistent critical-compromise indicator is
\begin{equation}
 p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}}
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 t\geq L-1
 \;\wedge\;
 \sum_{k=t-L+1}^{t} b_k^{\mathrm{crit}}=L
 \right].
 \label{eq:app-persistent-step}
\end{equation}
The special case \(L=2\) corresponds to critical compromise persisting
for two consecutive timesteps.

For a trajectory \(\tau\), the event-level ever-critical-breach
indicator is
\begin{equation}
 B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}>0
 \right].
 \label{eq:app-ever-critical}
\end{equation}
Critical dwell is
\begin{equation}
 D_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
 =
 \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}.
 \label{eq:app-critical-dwell}
\end{equation}
The first critical-hit time is
\begin{equation}
 T_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)
 =
 \min\{t:b_t^{\mathrm{crit}}=1\},
 \qquad
 T_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)=\infty
 \text{ if no critical breach occurs}.
 \label{eq:app-first-critical-time}
\end{equation}
When a finite mean first critical-hit time is reported, it is computed
only over trajectories with \(B_{\mathrm{crit}}(\tau)=1\); no-breach
trajectories are reported separately through \(B_{\mathrm{crit}}\). The
trajectory-level persistent critical-breach indicator is
\begin{equation}
 B_{\mathrm{persist}}^{(L)}(\tau)
 =
 \mathbb{1}\!\left[
 \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} p_{t,L}^{\mathrm{crit}}>0
 \right].
 \label{eq:app-persistent-trajectory}
\end{equation}
These variables support replay-level semantic analysis, event-style
constraint summaries, and diagnostic reporting. They are not added as
extra observations to the defender.

\subsection{Empirical Operational Acceptance}
\label{app:empirical-acceptance}

The population-level operational quantities are estimated by a fixed
rollout protocol. For \(N_{\mathrm{eval}}\) evaluation trajectories
\(\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}\), we estimate the operational
cost or violation statistic \(g_m(\pi)\) by
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{g}_m(\pi)
 =
 \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}
 \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{eval}}}
 c_m(\tau_j),
 \qquad
 \tau_j\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{M}}^\pi .
 \label{eq:app-empirical-g}
\end{equation}
Given operational limits \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), the empirical mean
violation is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{V}(\pi;\boldsymbol{\delta})
 =
 \frac{1}{M}
 \sum_{m=1}^{M}
 \left[\widehat{g}_m(\pi)-\delta_m\right]_+ .
 \label{eq:app-empirical-violation}
\end{equation}
Feasibility and violation in the paper are empirical quantities under
this evaluation protocol. They should not be interpreted as deployment
certificates for a production network.

If evaluation trajectories are independent and \(c_m(\tau)\in[0,1]\), a
one-sided Hoeffding bound gives, for any \(\alpha\in(0,1)\),
\begin{equation}
 g_m(\pi)
 \leq
 \widehat{g}_m(\pi)
 +
 \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{2N_{\mathrm{eval}}}}
 \quad
 \text{with probability at least }1-\alpha .
 \label{eq:app-hoeffding}
\end{equation}
For event-style semantic variables with zero observed events over
\(N_{\mathrm{event}}\) independent replay runs, the one-sided
Clopper--Pearson upper bound is
\begin{equation}
 p_m
 \leq
 1-\alpha^{1/N_{\mathrm{event}}}
 \qquad
 \text{with confidence }1-\alpha .
 \label{eq:app-zero-event-bound}
\end{equation}
Thus, observing no critical breach in finite replay is finite-protocol
evidence of a low event rate. It is not evidence of zero risk across all
executions or attacker variants.

\subsection{Candidate Records and Archive Sets}
\label{app:candidate-records}

DA-CPSL stores each evaluated candidate as
\begin{equation}
 x(\pi)
 =
 \left(
 \pi,
 \widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi),
 \widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi),
 \widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)
 \right).
 \label{eq:app-candidate-record}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\) estimates the optimized objective
returns, \(\widehat{\mathbf{g}}(\pi)\) estimates operational constraint
summaries, and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\pi)\) records semantic
analysis variables such as \(B_{\mathrm{crit}}\),
\(B_{\mathrm{persist}}^{(L)}\), \(T_{\mathrm{crit}}\), and
\(D_{\mathrm{crit}}\).

Let \(\Pi\) denote the set of archived policy identities. The raw archive
contains all evaluated candidates. The acceptable archive under limits
\(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) contains candidates satisfying the empirical
operational acceptance rule. The raw Pareto set and acceptable Pareto set
are therefore different subsets of the same evaluated archive. This
distinction is important because a policy dominated in raw objective
space can still be useful when the policies that dominate it fail
operational acceptance.

\subsection{Baseline Implementation Details}
\label{app:baseline-details}

This subsection provides the baseline details summarized in
Section~\ref{subsec:eval-methods}. All baselines are evaluated under the
same cyber-range instantiation, objective definitions, deployment
thresholds, and semantic analysis rules whenever applicable.

\paragraph{Stage-1 Candidate Set.}
This ablation evaluates the preference-diverse initialization stage
without Stage~2 Pareto extension. It tests whether the initial candidate
policy set alone is sufficient to support deployable selected policies.
Each Stage~1 policy is trained under a sampled preference vector and is
then treated as a preference-independent candidate during evaluation.

\paragraph{Weighted-Sum PPO.}
This scalarized baseline trains independent PPO policies under fixed
explicit preference vectors over the four objectives. It tests whether a
set of separately trained scalarized policies can substitute for an
evaluated candidate policy set. During evaluation, the learned policies
are treated as candidate policies and are assigned under the same
preference grid and deployment thresholds used for DA-CPSL.

\paragraph{Preference-Conditioned PPO.}
This baseline trains a single policy conditioned on the preference vector.
It tests whether direct preference conditioning can cover the
four-objective trade-off surface without maintaining a separate evaluated
record set. For candidate-set evaluation, the preference-conditioned
policy is executed over the same evaluation preference grid, and the
resulting evaluated policies or returns are used to compute
objective-space metrics.

\paragraph{Lagrangian PPO.}
This constrained single-policy baseline augments scalarized PPO training
with Lagrangian penalties for operational constraint violations. It tests
whether a single constrained policy can satisfy shared operational
limits under heterogeneous preferences. The learned policy is evaluated
under the same deployment thresholds as DA-CPSL.

\paragraph{Unconstrained Stage~2.}
This ablation uses the same Stage~2 Pareto-extension structure as
DA-CPSL, but removes acceptance-aware constraint shaping. It tests
whether raw Pareto extension alone is sufficient for operationally
deployable defense. The comparison with DA-CPSL isolates the role of
acceptance-aware shaping during extension.

\paragraph{Three-Objective DA-CPSL.}
This ablation removes Critical-Asset Safety from the objective vector
while keeping the remaining protocol matched. It tests whether the
critical-risk objective is necessary to represent deployable defense
trade-offs. The resulting policies are still evaluated with critical-risk
semantic analysis, so failures hidden by the three-objective training
signal can be observed during replay.

We do not mix older baselines that were not rerun under the unified
four-objective protocol into the main tables. Such comparisons would
conflate differences in objectives, thresholds, and semantic-analysis
rules.

\subsection{Metric Definitions and Assignment Protocol}
\label{app:metric-details}

This subsection gives the metric and assignment details summarized in
Section~\ref{subsec:eval-metrics}. The evaluation separates
objective-space candidate-set quality, operational deployability, and
replay-level cyber semantics.

\paragraph{Objective-space candidate-set metrics.}
We evaluate candidate-set quality with hypervolume, expected utility,
coverage ratio, the number of distinct assigned policies, and the number
of retained Pareto records. Hypervolume is computed on the empirical
Pareto front using a shared reference point fixed across methods.
Expected utility averages assignment utility over the evaluation
preference grid. The coverage ratio is the fraction of retained Pareto
records selected by at least one preference in the assignment grid.
These metrics describe objective-space quality; they do not imply
operational deployability.

\paragraph{Operational selection metrics.}
For deployment evaluation, buffer-based methods first select one
representative policy from the empirical Pareto front. The selector is
threshold-aware. Policies satisfying the shared business and cost
thresholds are preferred. Ties are broken by smaller total violation,
larger security return, and larger business-plus-cost return. We report
feasible rate, mean constraint violation, selected objective returns,
final critical compromised hosts, and critical-impact count.

\paragraph{Replay-level semantic analysis metrics.}
For selected policies, we perform replay-level semantic analysis using
the fixed replay protocol. The analysis reports ever-critical-breach
rate, persistent-critical-breach rate, critical dwell, first critical-hit
time, near-miss tiers, and phase-conditioned response behavior. Zero
observed critical breach is interpreted together with near-miss behavior
as evidence of pre-critical containment, not as evidence of risk
elimination.

\paragraph{Finite-sample interpretation of zero observed events.}
Let \(N\) be the number of replay episodes and suppose a critical event
is observed zero times. The one-sided Clopper--Pearson upper confidence
bound at confidence level \(1-\alpha\) is
\[
1-\alpha^{1/N}.
\]
In our main semantic analysis, the pooled replay count is
\(N=3\times 20=60\). Therefore, observing \(0/60\) events gives a
95\% one-sided upper bound of \(1-0.05^{1/60}\approx4.87\%\). This bound
does not prove that the event cannot occur. It only states that, under
the evaluated replay protocol, the event rate compatible with zero
observations is bounded at the reported confidence level.

\section{DA-CPSL Algorithmic Details}
\label{app:dacpsl-details}

\subsection{Acceptance-Aware Parent Selection}
\label{app:parent-selection}

At each extension round, DA-CPSL selects parent policies from the current
archive. If the acceptable Pareto set is non-empty, the parent source set
is
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_{\mathrm{src}}
 =
 \Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta}).
 \label{eq:app-parent-source-acceptable}
\end{equation}
If no acceptable Pareto candidate exists early in training, the parent
source set may fall back to the raw Pareto set,
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_{\mathrm{src}}
 =
 \Pi_{\mathrm{P}}(\Pi).
 \label{eq:app-parent-source-raw}
\end{equation}
This fallback is used only to continue exploration. A candidate selected
from the raw Pareto set is not eligible for deployment unless it later
passes operational acceptance.

Within \(\Pi_{\mathrm{src}}\), we use objective-space diversity to avoid
repeatedly extending crowded regions of the archive. Extreme candidates
for each objective are retained, and the remaining parents are ranked by
a crowd-distance-style score. When two candidates have comparable
coverage value, we prefer the one with smaller empirical violation and
lower critical-risk summary. The parent-selection rule therefore promotes
both objective diversity and operationally safer extension starting
points.

\subsection{Extension Surrogate}
\label{app:extension-surrogate}

Given a parent policy \(\pi_r\) and a target objective
\(\ell\in\mathcal{I}\), Stage~2 trains a child policy that improves
objective \(\ell\) while preserving non-target objectives and discouraging
operationally unacceptable behavior. Let
\begin{equation}
 s_i(\pi;\pi_r)
 =
 \widehat{J}_i(\pi)-\eta_i\widehat{J}_i(\pi_r),
 \qquad i\neq \ell,
 \label{eq:app-preservation-slack}
\end{equation}
where \(\eta_i\) is the parent-relative preservation coefficient used on
the stored return scale. A representative extension objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\theta)
 ={}&
 \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{\ell}(\theta)
 +
 \lambda_{\mathrm{bar}}
 \sum_{i\neq\ell}
 \log\!\left(s_i(\pi_\theta;\pi_r)+\epsilon\right)
 \\
 &-
 \lambda_{\mathrm{op}}
 \sum_m
 \left[\widehat{g}_m(\pi_\theta)-\delta_m\right]_+
 -
 \lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}
 \widehat{\psi}_{\mathrm{crit}}(\pi_\theta).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app-extension-surrogate}
\end{equation}
Here, \(\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{\ell}\) is the PPO-style clipped
surrogate for the target objective, the log barrier discourages degrading
non-target objectives below parent-relative thresholds, and the penalty
terms discourage operational violation and critical-risk behavior. This
surrogate is a training device. Final deployment eligibility is still
determined by post-update evaluation and acceptance filtering.

\subsection{Full DA-CPSL Workflow}
\label{app:full-workflow}

The full workflow is as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Preference-diverse initialization.} Sample a small set of
preference vectors \(\{\mathbf{w}_q\}_{q=1}^{Q}\). For each preference,
train a policy with scalarized PPO and evaluate the resulting policy
under the shared evaluation protocol. Store each evaluated record in the
archive \(\Pi\).

\item \textbf{Acceptance-aware Pareto extension.} For each extension
round: compute the raw Pareto set and the acceptable Pareto set; select
parent policies using Appendix~\ref{app:parent-selection}; train child
policies with Eq.~\ref{eq:app-extension-surrogate}; re-evaluate each
child policy; and insert the resulting evaluated record into \(\Pi\).

\item \textbf{Deployment-time assignment.} Given a preference vector
\(\mathbf{w}\) and limits \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), compute
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\). If this set
is empty, return \emph{infeasible}; otherwise return
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \pi^\star(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})
 \in
 \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\pi\in
 \Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})}
 \mathbf{w}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app-assignment}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Idealized Properties}
\label{app:idealized-properties}

This section records basic properties of the idealized formulation. They
are mathematical properties of the definitions used in the paper, not
formal guarantees of production-network safety.

\paragraph{Proposition D.1: Monotone calibration preserves dominance.}
Suppose the calibration map \(\mathcal{C}\) is coordinate-wise monotone.
If \(\pi_a\succ_J \pi_b\), then
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{J}(\pi_a))\) weakly dominates
\(\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{J}(\pi_b))\), with strict improvement in at least
one coordinate whenever \(\mathcal{C}\) is strictly monotone on that
coordinate.

\emph{Proof sketch.} Pareto dominance requires
\(J_i(\pi_a)\geq J_i(\pi_b)\) for every objective \(i\), with strict
inequality for at least one objective. Coordinate-wise monotonicity
preserves the weak inequalities. Strict monotonicity on a strictly
improved coordinate preserves strict improvement.

\paragraph{Proposition D.2: Assignment optimality within the acceptable Pareto set.}
If \(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\neq\emptyset\),
then the assignment rule in Eq.~\ref{eq:app-assignment} returns a policy
that maximizes calibrated linear utility over the acceptable Pareto set.

\emph{Proof sketch.} The result follows from the definition of
\(\pi^\star(\mathbf{w},\boldsymbol{\delta})\) as the maximizer of
\(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{J}}(\pi)\) over
\(\Pi_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{dep}}(\Pi,\boldsymbol{\delta})\). If the set
is empty, DA-CPSL returns \emph{infeasible} by definition.

\paragraph{Proposition D.3: Acceptance filtering enforces the specified empirical constraints.}
For any policy \(\pi\) in the empirical acceptable archive,
\(\widehat{g}_m(\pi)\leq\delta_m\) for all operational constraint indices
\(m\).

\emph{Proof sketch.} This follows from the definition of the acceptable
archive. The statement is empirical because the archive uses
\(\widehat{g}_m\) estimated under the evaluation protocol. Population-level
constraint satisfaction would require additional statistical assumptions
and confidence analysis.

competitive in conventional objective-space terms while repeatedly
entering critical compromise under semantic analysis.
